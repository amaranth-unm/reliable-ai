# Synthesis

**Version:** 1
**Notes included:** notes/S01-tadimalla.md (S01), notes/S02-klein.md (S02)
**Brief question:** How are the humanities relevant to using and teaching AI?

Neither source cites the other. S02 (v1 February 2025) predates S01 (December 2025), and S01's reference list does not include S02. Every connection below is therefore one I drew, marked "Read alongside …".

## Where they overlap

**P01: Technical design choices carry values, so the social dimension cannot be split off from the technical.**
- S01: its own emphasis is curricular. Separating "Analyze Implications of AI on Society" (Pillar 4) from technical understanding (Pillar 1) "impoverishes Pillar 1," because "any AI system worth developing will have social implications that stem directly from its technical specifications." Its example is the design choices in a skin-lesion classifier (pp. 10–11; also "sociotechnical system," p. 4).
- S02: its own emphasis is on research practice. "Any act of dataset creation is a political act"; there is "always a perspective encoded in the selection of data, the creation of annotations … and the construction of prompts" (p. 9). Bias in data is silences and absences that "can never be 'de-biased' or modeled away" (p. 8).
- Read alongside S01 and S02: both answer the same question, whether value judgments can be left out of the technical work. Both say no. S01 draws a teaching conclusion (don't teach Pillar 1 without Pillar 4); S02 draws a research conclusion (document the perspectives encoded in data).
- Who might disagree: S02 itself names the de-biasing, documentation and interpretability work it considers "necessary" but not enough (p. 8). Researchers who see bias as a fixable property of data would reject the "never" in S02. S01's claim is weaker and less likely to be contested.

**P02: Technical fields lack the expertise that the social and cultural side of AI needs.**
- S01: Pillar 4 is often minimized because it "goes well beyond the disciplinary training of many of those in computer science departments who are tasked with teaching AI Literacy"; S01 calls this de-emphasis "a mistake" (pp. 9, 15). S01 does not say who should teach it instead.
- S02: humanities expertise ("our knowledge about the past … our command of meaning-making practices") "has not been leveraged in generative AI research" (p. 2). Humanities scholars "must be brought to the table as equal partners," funded, and included in leadership (p. 14).
- Read alongside S01 and S02: the two passages address the same gap from different sides. S01 locates it in AI teaching and leaves open who fills it. S02 locates it in AI research and names humanities scholars as the answer. Interpretation: S02's answer could be read as a reply to S01's open question, but S02 makes no claim about teaching AI literacy. That extension is mine.
- Who might disagree: computer science instructors who already teach AI ethics. S02 also suggests that philosophy, the humanities field most present in technical venues, has been treated as a sufficient stand-in (p. 4); philosophers would contest that.

**P03: AI output should not be taken as knowledge at face value.**
- S01: users should ask "Where did this information come from? What biases are embedded within it? And how does it change how we know what we know?" (p. 15). Students using AI to find sources must still evaluate "credibility, relevance, or evidence of bias" (p. 12).
- S02: "Models make words, but people make meaning." Fluent output creates "the illusion that the model 'knows' something" (pp. 6–7). Humanists read model output "not for what they 'know,' but for what meaning their output elicits about the cultures from which they emerged" (p. 7).
- Read alongside S01 and S02: both address how people should treat what a model produces. Their answers differ in kind. S01 treats output as a claim to be verified (is it reliable, and where did it come from?). S02 treats output as a cultural artifact to be interpreted. Interpretation: S02's stance is the more distinctively humanistic of the two; S01's is closer to information literacy.
- Who might disagree: users and developers who treat LLMs as reliable knowledge tools for well-defined tasks.

## Where they differ

**P04: Do the humanities enter AI chiefly through ethics?**
- S01: the humanities appear as a source of ethical reasoning: "an interdisciplinary approach that draws from the humanities and social sciences to foster deep ethical reasoning" (p. 3). Its other humanities example is about cultural and historical context in a course using translation tools (p. 12).
- S02: "'ethics' and 'humanities' are not equivalent" (p. 14). The privileging of analytic philosophy at FAccT "conveniently mirror[s] the engineering impulse to propose a technical 'fix'" (p. 4). S02 locates the humanities' contribution in interpretation, contextualization and theorization (p. 3).
- Kind of difference: a difference of emphasis, not a debate. S01 never argues that the humanities equal ethics; it simply routes their contribution through ethics. But S02's argument would apply to that framing. Interpretation: this is the sharpest tension in the set for the brief. It bears on what "humanities" means when AI literacy programs invoke it.

**P05: Audience and purpose.**
- S01 addresses educators, students and institutions designing AI literacy curricula (pp. 1, 6, 9–10, 15).
- S02 addresses AI researchers, especially the FAccT community, who build and study models (pp. 2, 4, 14).
- Kind of difference: audience. It explains most of the gaps between the two: S01 says little about model building, and S02 says little about teaching (see P10–P11).

**P06: How central is the choice not to use AI?**
- S01 makes non-use a core element of agency: "AI Competency has to include knowing how and when not to use AI." It resists "top-down imposition of AI into all learning" (pp. 12–13).
- S02 does not discuss non-use as such. It models humanists building and fine-tuning small models for their own research (p. 9) and hopes for "designing new systems … when appropriate," while resisting "extractive and harmful AI systems" (p. 15).
- Kind of difference: emphasis, not disagreement. Both make engagement conditional. S01 foregrounds the user's right to refuse; S02 foregrounds the terms on which humanists should take part.

## What only one offers

Checked against both notes.

**P07: Only S01 offers a teaching framework.**
- Instructional value as the test for adopting a tool: "the extent to which a tool strengthens student thinking, deepens learning, and sustains or even extends meaningful educator-student interactions" (p. 6).
- The four pillars, with module-level learning outcomes for Pillars 3–4 (pp. 9–10, Tables 1–2).
- A historical analogy to the calculator for cognitive offloading (p. 7).
- Respecting non-users (pp. 12–13).
- AI ethics framed as civic education, with storytelling, listening and empathy as "counters to synthetic text" (pp. 13–15).

**P08: Only S02 offers an account of what humanities research is and what its methods contribute to AI.**
- A working definition of humanities research and its methods (pp. 2–3).
- Two senses of "culture" applied to training data and models (pp. 7–8).
- Archival theories of silence and absence applied to dataset bias (p. 8).
- Small, curated models built for humanities research (p. 9).
- Humanistic data documentation: data archaeologies, data narratives, data essays (p. 10).
- Consent, licensing and Indigenous data sovereignty (p. 11).
- Histories of capitalism and universalism applied to compute and AGI (pp. 12–13).
- The warning against "humanities extraction," with its funding demands (p. 14).

## For using AI and for teaching AI

**P09: For using AI.**
- S01 offers general habits for any user:
  - questioning outputs and verifying claims (pp. 3–4, 15);
  - evaluating AI-recommended sources (p. 12);
  - deliberately deciding not to use AI (pp. 12–13).
- S02 offers humanities researchers a stance and some practices:
  - reading output as cultural evidence (p. 7);
  - Table 1's next steps, such as "Clarify which definition of culture you are using" and "Ask whose knowledge your data or model is attempting to represent" (pp. 6, 11);
  - examples of small, fine-tuned models for humanities research (p. 9);
  - data documentation (p. 10).
- Interpretation: neither gives tool-level guidance for a humanities researcher using a chatbot in everyday work (reading, translating, transcribing, drafting). S02 addresses builders; S01 addresses students and citizens.

**P10: For teaching AI.**
- Almost all of the set's teaching content comes from S01 (P07). Its only humanities-specific teaching example is the translation case (p. 12).
- S02 does not address teaching. It touches on it only in passing:
  - "digital pedagogy" as a keyword (p. 1);
  - AI ethics syllabi that exclude humanities readings (pp. 4–5);
  - a citation to the MLA-CCCC working papers (p. 5);
  - humanities researchers "teach more and are compensated less" (p. 14).
- So the brief's requirement for teaching sources currently rests on one source, and that source treats the humanities only briefly (S01 pp. 3, 12).

## Gaps

**P11: What neither source addresses.**
- Discipline-specific teaching, such as assignments or classroom practice in literature, history or languages. S01's examples are general, and S02 has none.
- How humanities researchers use AI tools day to day (see P09).
- Students' perspectives or experiences.
- Writing pedagogy, which is the chief classroom pressure point in humanities teaching. Neither source discusses it.

**P12: What the set over-represents.**
- Both sources are arXiv preprints (S01 v1; S02 v2, marked "To be added if accepted," p. 24). Neither is peer-reviewed in this form.
- Both are by US-based author teams.
- S02's humanities are mainly digital humanities, literary studies, history and Black studies.
- S01's team is mostly from education and computing, with two authors from humanities and social sciences (p. 1).
- So far there are no sources from humanities journals or professional bodies. S03 (MLA-CCCC) and S04 (PMLA) should address this.
