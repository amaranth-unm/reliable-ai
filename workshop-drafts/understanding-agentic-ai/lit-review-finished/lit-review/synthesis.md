**Brief question:** How are the humanities relevant to using and teaching AI?
**Page numbers:** These are PDF pages. For S04, PDF pp. 1–5 are journal pp. 548–552.

**Links the authors make themselves.** There are two, both thin, and neither is a direct engagement with another source in this set:
- S02 mentions the MLA-CCCC task force, which "published a set of working papers on AI and writing," as an example of digital humanists leading on AI (p. 5). Its reference [13] points to the task force's web page, not to S03, and S02 does not discuss S03's content.
- S02 cites Kirschenbaum and Raley's *PMLA* essay (ref. [106], p. 5). That essay is the prompt S04 responds to (S04 p. 3), but S02 does not cite S04.

No other source cites another:
- S01's references do not include S02, S03 or S04.
- S03's works cited do not include S04.

So every overlap and difference below is one I drew, marked "Read alongside …".

## Where they overlap

**P01: Technical design choices carry values, so the social dimension cannot be split off from the technical.** *(unchanged)*
- S01: its own emphasis is curricular. Separating "Analyze Implications of AI on Society" (Pillar 4) from technical understanding (Pillar 1) "impoverishes Pillar 1," because "any AI system worth developing will have social implications that stem directly from its technical specifications." Its example is a skin-lesion classifier (pp. 10–11; also "sociotechnical system," p. 4).
- S02: its own emphasis is on research practice. "Any act of dataset creation is a political act"; there is "always a perspective encoded in the selection of data, the creation of annotations … and the construction of prompts" (p. 9). Bias is silences and absences that "can never be 'de-biased' or modeled away" (p. 8).
- Read alongside S01 and S02: both answer the same question, whether value judgments can be left out of the technical work, and both say no. S01 draws a teaching conclusion; S02 draws a research conclusion.
- Who might disagree: S02 names de-biasing and interpretability work that it considers "necessary" but not enough (p. 8). Researchers who see bias as fixable would reject S02's "never."
- S03 and S04 do not address this question directly, so they are not added.

**P02: The expertise that AI's challenges need lies partly outside technical fields, in the humanities.** *(changed: S03 and S04 added; claim reworded)*
- S01: Pillar 4 "goes well beyond the disciplinary training of many of those in computer science departments who are tasked with teaching AI Literacy" (pp. 9, 15). S01 does not say who should teach it instead.
- S02: humanities expertise "has not been leveraged in generative AI research" (p. 2). Humanities scholars "must be brought to the table as equal partners," and they must be funded and included in leadership (p. 14).
- S03: institutions should recognize that AI research happens across the humanities and not only in STEM (pp. 17–18). They should draw on literary and film criticism, "particularly in science fiction," to understand how popular attitudes to AI formed (p. 19).
- S04: "Humanities scholars, especially those studying interpretive theories and methodologies, are uniquely equipped to address the challenges that large AI models pose" (p. 3). Engineers now have to work through "the rhetoric of natural language" rather than "the mechanics of code" (p. 2).
- Read alongside S01–S04: all four say that technical fields alone are not enough. They locate the gap in different places: teaching (S01), research (S02), institutions (S03) and disciplinary relations (S04). Interpretation: applying S02's call for equal partnership to S01's open question of who should teach Pillar 4 is my extension, not either author's.
- Who might disagree: computer science instructors who already teach AI ethics. Philosophers would contest S02's suggestion that philosophy has been a convenient stand-in (p. 4). Within the set, S04 qualifies the claim: it says many humanists' early responses have been "redundant with existing social thought or manifestly reactionary" (p. 1). Being equipped is not the same as responding well.

**P03: AI output should not be taken as knowledge at face value.** *(changed: S03 and S04 added)*
- S01: users should ask "Where did this information come from? What biases are embedded within it?" (p. 15). They should evaluate AI-recommended sources for "credibility, relevance, or evidence of bias" (p. 12).
- S03: GAI systems "are not thinkers but statistical models," and their output can be "factually or demonstrably untrue" even when it looks plausible (p. 5). Students should evaluate output for accuracy, relevance and bias, and should check references against library databases or the print record (p. 6).
- S02: "Models make words, but people make meaning." Fluent output creates "the illusion that the model 'knows' something" (pp. 6–7). Humanists read output for "what meaning their output elicits about the cultures from which they emerged" (p. 7).
- S04: intuitive prompting effects "imply (wrongly) that LLMs have mental states" (p. 2). Models "fabricat[e] evidence and behave unpredictably" (p. 3).
- Read alongside S01–S04: all four warn against mistaking fluent output for knowledge, but their responses differ in kind. S01 and S03 treat output as a claim to verify, which is close to information literacy. S02 treats it as a cultural artifact to interpret. S04 offers a diagnosis rather than a practice. On S04's further view of what this means for human subjectivity, see P13.
- Who might disagree: users and developers who rely on LLMs as dependable tools for well-defined tasks.

**P06: Not using AI is a legitimate choice, though not a free one.** *(changed: moved from "Where they differ" to here; S03 added; reworded)*
- S01: "AI Competency has to include knowing how and when not to use AI." It resists "top-down imposition of AI into all learning" (pp. 12–13).
- S03: critical AI literacy is "neither for nor against the use of AI" (p. 21). Instructors' choice to opt out should be respected (p. 11), and programs should model "student agency to not use GAI" (p. 16). But S03 adds qualifications: when an instructor opts out, "the burden of teaching students to use these technologies responsibly is shifted to other classes and instructors, including those in more precarious positions" (p. 11). GAI is embedded in everyday software, so it cannot simply be avoided (p. 5). Ignoring it "is not a sustainable position" (p. 9).
- S02 does not discuss non-use. It models humanists building small models (p. 9) while resisting "extractive and harmful AI systems" (p. 15). S04 does not discuss non-use either; it urges readers to "hack, pirate, and otherwise mess with" proprietary models (p. 5).
- Read alongside S01 and S03: both make non-use a legitimate, deliberate choice. S03 alone counts its cost to colleagues. S02 and S04 put their emphasis on taking part on better terms. This is a difference of emphasis, not a disagreement.
- Who might disagree: advocates of university-wide AI adoption, such as the mandates S01 cites (p. 2).

**P14: Prompting is a rhetorical practice, which humanists already know how to do.** *(new)*
- S03: students should prompt by drawing on their existing rhetorical skills (p. 8). It lists "prompt engineering as a rhetorical practice" among program-level outcomes (p. 13).
- S04: "The new field of prompt engineering can be understood as the rhetorical study of how AI models respond to inputs." Its examples are "Let's think step by step" and gibberish jailbreak strings (p. 2).
- Read alongside S03 and S04: the same claim, made independently (neither cites the other). It is one of the most concrete answers in the set to what the humanities offer people who use AI.
- Who might disagree: S01, within this set. It warns that "only training students to use current AI models" leaves their knowledge obsolete, and it cites reports that prompt engineering is already obsolete as a speciality (p. 10). It wants to reframe AI literacy "away from prompt optimization" (p. 14), and it warns of graduates "fluent in prompt engineering but illiterate in democracy" (p. 15). Interpretation: S01 is attacking prompting as tool training, not prompting as rhetoric. So the disagreement may be smaller than it looks, but S01 does not make that distinction itself.

## Where they differ

**P04: Do the humanities enter AI chiefly through ethics?** *(changed: S03 and S04 added; S01 now stands alone)*
- S01: the humanities appear as a source of ethical reasoning, "an interdisciplinary approach that draws from the humanities and social sciences to foster deep ethical reasoning" (p. 3).
- S02: "'ethics' and 'humanities' are not equivalent" (p. 14). It locates the contribution in "interpretation, contextualization, and theorization" (p. 3).
- S03: defines AI literacy through the multiliteracies framing, as critical, rhetorical and ethical judgment (pp. 5, 21 n1). Rhetoric and literacy are its center of gravity (see P14).
- S04: locates the contribution in interpretive theory, rhetoric and aesthetics (pp. 2–3). It treats several ethical and political critiques as redundant or reactionary (pp. 4–5).
- Kind of difference: emphasis, not a debate. S01 never claims the humanities equal ethics, and S03 and S04 do not argue the point. But taken together, three sources locate the humanities' contribution in interpretation, rhetoric, literacy and theory, while S01 routes it through ethics. Interpretation: this matters for the brief, because S01 is the source most focused on teaching AI literacy.

**P05: Audience and purpose.** *(changed: S03 and S04 added)*
- S01: educators, students and institutions designing general AI literacy curricula (pp. 1, 6, 9–10, 15).
- S02: AI researchers, especially the FAccT community (pp. 2, 4, 14).
- S03: language, literature and writing educators, programs, academic support units and institutions (pp. 5–20).
- S04: literary scholars reading *PMLA* (p. 1).
- Kind of difference: audience. S01 and S03 tell teachers what to do, S02 tells builders what to do, and S04 tells humanists how to think. These audiences explain most of the gaps between the sources (P09–P11).

**P13: What does poststructuralist theory say about machine-made language and human meaning?** *(new)*
- S02: drawing on Barthes and on semiotics, it argues that meaning comes from readers and communities, not from a speaker's intention. "Meaning is always people-fueled," and AI "knowing" is "a false solution" (pp. 6–7).
- S04: following Underwood, it says LLMs give empirical proof of the poststructuralist thesis that language "actively determines the contours of the thinkable." It concludes: "The greater mistake is not to see machine-written texts as expressive of consciousness but to see our own subjectivity as uninvolved with the mechanistic underpinnings of language" (p. 3).
- Read alongside S02 and S04: both draw on the same body of theory, and both deny that models know or intend. But they push it in different directions. S02 uses it to place meaning with people and communities. S04 uses it to unsettle the idea of a human subject who stands apart from language's mechanisms.
- Kind of difference: possibly a real disagreement, but not a debate the authors are having. Interpretation: one could read the two as compatible, since S04 is talking about human subjectivity, not model knowledge. The difference lies in what each wants humanists to defend.

**P15: Should creators' consent and ownership constrain training data?** *(new)*
- S02: "preserving the ability of writers and artists to create their art and be fairly compensated for it should remain paramount" (p. 11). It also says that excluding sources has downstream costs, and it cites fair-use routes for research approvingly (p. 11). Its test: "ask whose knowledge your data or model is attempting to represent, whether you are authorized to make use of it" (p. 11). It points to Indigenous and community data licenses as models (p. 11).
- S04: copyright objections to AI are "surprisingly reactionary," a "new copyright fundamentalism" that reverses the left's free-culture commitments (p. 4). It asks how training differs "in principle" from a person reading in a library (p. 5).
- S03: takes no position. It points administrators to the Library Copyright Alliance's distinction between ingesting copyrighted material for training under fair use and judging whether output infringes (p. 20).
- Kind of difference: a real disagreement between S02 and S04 on the same question, though narrower than it first appears. S02 also values open access for research (p. 11), and S04's target is copyright law, not community consent, which it does not discuss. Neither cites the other.

**P16: Are environmental and labor costs a distinctive objection to generative AI?** *(new)*
- S02: "The cost to achieve minimal gains in performance is now growing exponentially." The environmental and human costs of minerals, data centers and annotation labor "are devastating" (p. 12). It places them within histories of capitalism and colonial labor (p. 12).
- S01: AI-associated data servers may use "as much energy as the entire nation of Japan by 2030." It counts this among the concerns that "are real" (p. 5).
- S04: environmental and labor politics are "urgent concerns," but large AI models "do not transform either." Per-use costs are trivial by comparison, and AI's annotation labor "differs neither in quality nor in scale" from earlier digital labor. These critiques "bear no special relation to large AI models per se." S04 concedes that "continuing growth of model size and usage will increase their effects" (p. 4).
- Kind of difference: a real disagreement about scale and specificity. S04 argues against Bender et al., whom S02 cites approvingly (S02 ref. [20]), but that is a shared reference, not an exchange between these authors. Interpretation: S02 and S04 both place these harms in longer histories. They draw opposite conclusions from that: S02 says the history helps people resist, and S04 says it shows the harms are not new.
- S03 does not take this up; it mentions "labor frameworks" only in passing (p. 9).

## What only one offers

Each item was checked against all four notes.

**P07: Only S01 offers the four-pillar framework and its civic framing.** *(weakened: "a teaching framework" and "non-users" are no longer unique to S01; see P17 and P06)*
- Instructional value as the test for adopting a tool (p. 6).
- The four pillars, with module-level outcomes for Pillars 3–4 (pp. 9–10, Tables 1–2).
- The calculator analogy for cognitive offloading (p. 7).
- AI ethics framed as civic education, with storytelling, listening and empathy as "counters to synthetic text" (pp. 13–15).
- S03 also offers learning outcomes and a framework for teaching (P17), and it also respects non-use (P06). Only the items listed here are unique to S01.

**P08: Only S02 offers an account of what humanities research is and how its methods apply to building AI.** *(changed: consent and licensing removed from the unique list, because S04 and S03 also address training-data rights; see P15)*
- A working definition of humanities research and its methods (pp. 2–3).
- Two senses of "culture" applied to data and models (pp. 7–8).
- Archival theories of silence applied to dataset bias (p. 8).
- Small, curated models built for humanities research (p. 9).
- Humanistic data documentation (p. 10).
- Indigenous and community data licensing (p. 11).
- AGI read as universalism (p. 13).
- The warning against "humanities extraction," with its funding demands (p. 14).

**P17: Only S03 offers discipline-specific, multi-level guidance for teaching in language, literature and writing.** *(new)*
- Recommendations at four levels: students, educators, programs and departments, and institutions. These include writing centers and tutors, with no "surveillance of and reporting on learners' GAI use" (p. 15), and graduate student instructors (p. 12).
- Disclosure practices, up to "the full record of prompts and outputs" (p. 7).
- Instructors modeling transparency, and caution against LLM grading and feedback (p. 10).
- Bias against multilingual writers and the misuse of AI detectors (p. 11).
- Folding AI literacy into existing outcomes rather than adding it on, with backward course design (pp. 13–14).
- Ongoing faculty development rather than "the one-shot workshop" (p. 16).
- Institutional caution about AI in hiring and admissions (pp. 19–20).

**P18: Only S04 offers a case for literary theory and aesthetics as the humanities' answer to AI.** *(new)*
- Large models reverse the usual division of labor between the disciplines: humanities discourse becomes computable, and engineering becomes rhetorical (pp. 1–2).
- Model opacity and tokenization as subjects for humanistic analysis (p. 2).
- LLMs as empirical confirmation of theory (p. 3).
- A call to return to aesthetic judgment: "With the issue of quantity rendered moot, that of quality remains" (p. 3).
- A classification of political critiques into redundant ones, reactionary ones and genuinely new harms such as bespoke propaganda, deepfakes and opaque algorithmic decisions (pp. 4–5).

## For using AI and for teaching AI

**P09: For using AI.** *(changed: S03 and S04 added; the interpretation is revised)*
- **Verification habits:**
  - S01 says to question outputs, verify claims and evaluate sources, and to decide deliberately when not to use AI (pp. 3–4, 12–13, 15).
  - S03 says the same and adds checking references against the print record (p. 6), reading the terms of service (pp. 6–7), and breaking a task into steps to judge "at each phase what GAI has to offer" (p. 7).
- **Disclosure:** S03 gives concrete practices, from footnotes to full prompt records (p. 7).
- **Prompting as rhetoric:** S03 (pp. 8, 13) and S04 (p. 2); see P14.
- **Interpretive stances:**
  - S02 reads output as cultural evidence (p. 7) and offers Table 1's questions (p. 6).
  - S04 warns that apparent mental states are an illusion (p. 2).
- **Building:** S02's small, fine-tuned models for humanities research (p. 9) and its data documentation (p. 10).
- Interpretation: The set now gives a humanist user more than version 1 did, especially through S03 and the shared rhetoric point (P14). It still offers nothing on everyday research uses such as reading, transcription or translation at scale. Per the S03 note, none of the sources addresses agentic tools that act on files or systems.

**P10: For teaching AI.** *(changed: S03 added; S01 is no longer the only substantial teaching source)*
- **S03** is the most detailed teaching source, and the only one specific to humanities disciplines (P17).
- **S01** gives a general, cross-disciplinary framework (P07). Its one humanities example is the translation case (p. 12).
- **S04** offers one paragraph: ask advanced students "not for longer essays but for better ideas," since defining "better" is "a genuine intellectual task" (p. 3). It also supplies conceptual and legal material that could be taught (pp. 2–5).
- **S02** does not address teaching beyond passing mentions (pp. 1, 4–5, 14).
- Read alongside S03 and S04: both respond to how easily AI produces text by shifting what is assessed. S03 shifts the focus to process and disclosure (pp. 7, 11–12); S04 shifts it to quality of ideas (p. 3). These are different answers, not a disagreement.
- The brief requires at least two sources about teaching. That is now met by S01 and S03.

## Gaps

**P11: What no source addresses.** *(changed: writing pedagogy removed, since S03 covers it; other gaps refined)*
- Classroom practice in humanities disciplines other than writing, language and literature, such as history, philosophy or art history, at the level of assignments.
- Students' own perspectives or experiences.
- Evidence of what works. S01 and S03 make recommendations without evidence of their effect; the brief does not require it, but the gap is worth stating.
- Agentic or multi-step AI tools (per the S03 note).
- Everyday research use by humanists (see P09).
- Recency: S03 describes the state of things in summer 2024 (p. 4), and S04 was written in 2024. Claims about what the tools can do may have dated.

**P12: What the set over-represents.** *(changed)*
- **Venues:** two arXiv preprints (S01, S02) and two MLA publications. S03 is © MLA, and S04's *PMLA* is published on behalf of the MLA. That is within the brief's limit of two per venue, but literary and writing studies dominate the humanities in this set.
- **One circle:** Matthew Kirschenbaum is an author of S03 (p. 2). His essay with Raley is the prompt S04 answers (S04 p. 3). S02 cites him twice (refs. [105], [106]). The set may reflect one network's framing of the question.
- **Geography:** all four author teams are US-based.
- **Thin fields:** philosophy appears mainly as S02's foil (p. 4). History and the arts appear only through S02's examples. The set has no non-Anglophone perspective.
