- **PDF:** pdfs/S02-klein.pdf
- **Pages read:** 1–24, all pages. I read pp. 1–16 as page images, including Table 1 (p. 6). Pages 17–24 I read from the PDF's text layer: pp. 17–23 are the rest of the reference list and p. 24 is the endmatter. Page numbers below are PDF pages; they match the printed page numbers.
- **Kind of work:** A position paper, a set of "provocations" modeled on boyd & Crawford's "Critical Questions for Big Data" (p. 2). The authors call it "primarily a literature review and theory contribution" (p. 24). It is addressed to AI researchers, especially the FAccT community (pp. 2, 4).

## The argument in its own terms

Generative AI affects many groups of people, yet "the disciplinary inputs to its development have been surprisingly narrow," and humanities researchers are "currently underrepresented in AI development" (p. 1). The authors write as digital humanists. They argue that humanities expertise ("our knowledge about the past, our ability to conduct detailed analyses within and between cultures, and our command of meaning-making practices") has not been used in generative AI research (p. 2). They offer eight provocations, each with a recommended next step (Table 1, p. 6). They close by warning against "humanities extraction," where technical fields take up humanities ideas while the disciplines that produce them go unfunded and uncredited (pp. 13–15).

## Key ideas

1. **What humanities research is and does.** The authors define humanities research as "an investigation of the human—of people and groups, and the cultural objects they create" (p. 2). Its methods are "interpretation, contextualization, and theorization" (p. 3), and it builds "systems-level analyses from specific objects of culture," which can include a large language model (p. 2). Its goal is rarely a simple conclusion but "a deeper understanding of the 'big question'" (p. 3). The authors reject computer-science accounts of humanistic thinking as "basically a style or mode" (p. 3).
2. **"Models make words, but people make meaning."** Drawing on semiotics, Barthes and Black studies, the authors argue that meaning is made by readers and communities, not by a speaker's intention. Fluent output can create "the illusion that the model 'knows' something" (pp. 5–7). Humanists analyze model output "not for what they 'know,' but for what meaning their output elicits about the cultures from which they emerged" (p. 7).
3. **Culture, the archive and representativeness.** "Culture" can mean a group of people or the objects and expressions they produce. Training data and models are themselves expressions of culture, so researchers should say which sense they mean (pp. 7–8). Datasets can never be "representative." Using archival and Black feminist theory (Hartman, Fuentes, Johnson's "null value"), the authors treat bias as silences and absences that "can never be 'de-biased' or modeled away" (p. 8). "Any act of dataset creation is a political act" (p. 9).
4. **Smaller, curated models and richer data documentation.** "Bigger models are not always better models." Humanists value small, curated datasets. The authors cite examples of small or fine-tuned models built for humanities research: Irish migration studies, Malay-language newspapers, and an LLM trained only on nineteenth-century English (p. 9). They recommend documenting the "conceptual and cultural characteristics" of training data, using methods such as data archaeologies, data narratives and data essays (p. 10).
5. **Openness, capital and universalism.** Openness "is not an easy fix." The authors' test is to "ask whose knowledge your data or model is attempting to represent, whether you are authorized to make use of it, and whether your work might result in future harm" (p. 11). They place compute concentration within histories of capitalism and data colonialism (p. 12). They read the drive for AGI as "the latest iteration of universalism's false promise," drawing on Charles Mills, Cedric Robinson and Ramon Amaro (p. 13).

## What the authors say is new, and what they argue against

- **Claimed contribution:**
  - Eight new provocations for generative AI research (pp. 1–2).
  - "A working definition of humanities research," with a summary of its theories and methods applied to AI (p. 1).
  - A demand that humanities work "should have a direct impact on the development of generative AI," since such work "is rarely read or cited beyond humanities fields" (p. 5).
- **Argues against:**
  - The "epistemology of ignorance" in corporate AI research (p. 1, after Charles Mills).
  - Systems that serve a "universal user" or "fix" social problems (pp. 1–2).
  - The privileging of analytic philosophy as the stand-in for the humanities at FAccT. The authors say it "conveniently mirror[s] the engineering impulse to propose a technical 'fix'" (p. 4) and that "'ethics' and 'humanities' are not equivalent" (p. 14).
  - The idea that bigger is better, and the pursuit of AGI (pp. 9, 13).
  - Treating training data as undifferentiated "oil" (p. 10).
  - Corporate spokespeople who have "usurped" the national conversation about AI (p. 5).
  - "Humanities extraction" and "elite capture" (p. 14, after Táíwò).

## For using AI

The paper is addressed mainly to people who build and study AI, not to people who use it in their research. It does offer the following:
- The suggested next steps in Table 1 (p. 6). Most apply to anyone working with models, for example "Clarify which definition of culture you are using" and "Ask whose knowledge your data or model is attempting to represent."
- Examples of humanities researchers building or fine-tuning small models for their own research (p. 9).
- A reading of model output as evidence about the cultures it came from (p. 7).
- Ways to document data (p. 10) and to handle copyright and consent, including HathiTrust, DMCA exemptions and Indigenous data licenses (p. 11).
- Practical advice for collaborations: treat humanities scholars "as equal partners," fund them, include them in leadership, and cite relevant humanities experts "outside of the ACL Anthology or arXiv" (pp. 14–15).

Interpretation: For humanities researchers who use AI, the paper's value is mainly in showing which of their existing methods (interpretation, contextualization, archival practice, data critique) bear on AI. It gives little instruction on using AI tools.

## For teaching AI

Not addressed as a topic. The paper only touches on teaching:
- It lists "digital pedagogy" as a keyword (p. 1).
- It mentions pedagogical work integrating critical AI into the classroom, and Raji et al.'s finding that AI ethics syllabi exclude humanities readings (pp. 4–5).
- It cites the MLA-CCCC task force working papers (p. 5, ref. [13]).
- It notes that humanities researchers "teach more and are compensated less" (p. 14).

None of these is developed into guidance for teaching.

## Questions

- The wording of the provocations differs between the abstract and Table 1/the section headings. For example, "Generative AI requires an expanded definition of culture" (p. 1) becomes "AI requires an understanding of culture" (pp. 6–7), and "Generative AI can never be representative" (p. 1) becomes "AI can never be 'representative'" (pp. 6, 8). Quote whichever version is relevant and cite its page.
- The claim that philosophy has been privileged at FAccT because it mirrors engineering habits (p. 4) is likely to be contested, by philosophers and others. The authors hedge it themselves: "often (but not always e.g. …)."
- Who is the audience for the "humanities" here? Most examples come from digital humanities, literary studies, history and Black studies. Whether the claims hold for humanists who don't do computational work is not discussed.
- Interpretation: "AI can never be representative" may read to technical audiences as a counsel of despair. The authors' answer is to document perspectives rather than claim representativeness (p. 9). Whether that is enough is open.
