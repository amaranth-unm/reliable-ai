# S01 — Tadimalla et al., "Comprehensive AI Literacy"

- **Citation:** Sri Yash Tadimalla, Gordon Hull, Justin Cary, Jordan Register, Daniel Maxwell, David Pugalee, and Tina Heafner, "Comprehensive AI Literacy: The Case for Centering Human Agency." arXiv preprint 2512.16656v1 (December 2025). The PDF prints no venue or date.
- **PDF:** pdfs/S01-tadimalla.pdf
- **Pages read:** 1–23, all pages. I read pp. 1–16 as page images, including Fig. 1 (p. 3), Fig. 2 (p. 8) and Tables 1–2 (pp. 9–10). Pages 17–23 are only the rest of the reference list, which I read from the PDF's text layer. Page numbers below are PDF pages; they match the printed page numbers.
- **Kind of work:** A position paper that sets out a curricular framework. The authors call it a "position paper" (p. 1). It contains no study or data of its own.

## The argument in its own terms

AI literacy is being defined around operational skill and workforce readiness, and this is producing "a dangerous literacy gap" in critical and ethical reasoning (p. 1). The authors propose "comprehensive AI literacy" centered on human agency, "the empowered capacity for intentional, critical, and responsible choice" (pp. 1, 15). For students, this includes the choice not to use AI; for teachers, it means designing learning around instructional values "rather than ceding pedagogical control to a tool" (p. 1). Because AI is sociotechnical, the authors say, AI literacy cannot be separated from ethics and civic education (pp. 11, 13, 15).

## Key ideas

1. **Literacy means interrogating AI, not just using it.** Comprehensive AI literacy is a model that "braids technical competence with ethical stewardship and the cultivation of human agency" (p. 2). Without it, education produces naive users, excluded non-users, "uninformed AI champions" and "uninformed AI opponents" (p. 2). The authors argue this "requires an interdisciplinary approach that draws from the humanities and social sciences to foster deep ethical reasoning" (p. 3). This is the paper's only explicit claim about the humanities as such.
2. **Judge AI tools by instructional value.** The authors define instructional value as "the extent to which a tool strengthens student thinking, deepens learning, and sustains or even extends meaningful educator-student interactions" (p. 6). The question for teachers shifts from "Can we use this?" to "Will this enhance learning in ways that matter?" (p. 6). The paper's one humanities-course example: translation tools can give multilingual learners access to complex texts, but their value "depends on whether students still deeply engage with the cultural and historical context of those texts" (p. 12).
3. **The four pillars, with the fourth too often dropped.** Following Tadimalla and Maher (2025), the paper sorts literacy efforts into four pillars: (1) technical scope, (2) interaction, (3) critical, ethical and responsible use, and (4) implications of AI for society (p. 9). The authors say Pillar 4 is often minimized because it "goes well beyond the disciplinary training of many of those in computer science departments who are tasked with teaching AI Literacy," and they call that "a mistake" (pp. 9, 15). They argue social implications are part of the technical dimension, using the example of design choices in a skin-lesion classifier (pp. 10–11). Module-level learning outcomes for Pillars 3 and 4 are in Tables 1–2 (pp. 9–10).
4. **Respect non-users.** Choosing not to use AI is a core part of agency (p. 12). Drawing on Wyatt (2003) and Satchell & Dourish (2009), the authors reject the narrative that treats non-users as "lagging adopters" (p. 12). They argue that "AI Competency has to include knowing how and when not to use AI," and that it is "far too early for university-wide, top-down imposition of AI into all learning" (pp. 12–13).
5. **AI ethics as civic education.** The ethical component must be "outward facing" (p. 14). The authors list "storytelling, listening, and empathy" as "essential counters to synthetic text and fabricated content," and name verification, informed skepticism and epistemic vigilance as habits of mind (p. 14, citing Heafner & Maxwell's "CIVIC pillars," in press). They warn of graduates "fluent in prompt engineering but illiterate in democracy" (p. 15).

## What the authors say is new, and what they argue against

- **Claimed contribution:** The "AI Literacy, Fluency, and Competency frameworks described in this paper" (p. 1) and a framing of AI literacy that centers human agency (p. 15). The paper also says comprehensive AI literacy builds "on previous efforts" (p. 2), and it takes the four pillars from Tadimalla and Maher (2025) (p. 9).
- **Argues against:**
  - The workforce and competitiveness vision of AI literacy in Executive Order 14277, which the paper sets out "to challenge, build upon, and expand" (p. 3).
  - Teaching AI as a "black box" (p. 2).
  - Techno-determinism and techno-solutionism (p. 5).
  - Industry- and policy-driven narratives that everyone must learn to use AI better (p. 12).
  - Top-down mandates such as university-wide AI integration (pp. 2, 12, 15).
  - AI literacy efforts that omit Pillar 4 (p. 9).

## For using AI

People who use AI should treat it "as a tool to be wielded with purpose and skepticism—to know how to question its outputs, verify its claims, and recognize contexts where human judgment, empathy, and moral reasoning are irreplaceable" (pp. 3–4). On AI-assisted research, the authors say AI "may help a user locate relevant sources," but the value lies in evaluating "credibility, relevance, or evidence of bias within AI-recommended sources" (p. 12). Users should ask "Where did this information come from? What biases are embedded within it? And how does it change how we know what we know?" (p. 15). The decision not to use AI is part of professional practice, citing Cha & Wong (2025) (pp. 12–13).

Interpretation: The advice for users is addressed to students and citizens. It does not specifically address researchers in humanities disciplines.

## For teaching AI

This is the paper's main concern.
- Instructional value as the test for adopting a tool (pp. 6, 12).
- A calculator analogy for the benefits and risks of cognitive offloading (p. 7).
- Distinctions among literacy, fluency and competency (pp. 7–8, Fig. 2).
- Module-level learning outcomes for ethics, integrity and authorship, public perception, the future of work, and policy (Tables 1–2, pp. 9–10).
- Teaching non-use and the reasons for it (p. 13).
- Civic pedagogies such as storytelling, listening, empathy and the CIVIC habits (p. 14).
- Respect for teachers' agency: educators and students should decide "when, to what extent, and when not to use AI … through dialogue" (p. 15).

## Questions

- Interpretation: The humanities appear explicitly only twice: the "humanities and social sciences" sentence (p. 3) and the translation example (p. 12). The paper does not say which humanities disciplines or methods it means. The case for the humanities has to be inferred from its emphasis on ethics, history (the calculator, Luddites, pp. 7, 13) and interpretation.
- The abstract promises "AI Literacy, Fluency, and Competency frameworks" (p. 1), but the body defines the three terms (pp. 7–8) and then develops only literacy, through the four pillars. Fig. 2 (p. 8), which maps roles onto the pillars and the three levels, is not explained in the text.
- Who teaches Pillar 4? The authors say computer science instructors are often unprepared for it (pp. 9, 15) but do not say who should teach it instead.
- Some empirical claims rest on preprints or news reports, for example the MIT brain-activity study, Kosmyna et al. 2025, an arXiv preprint (p. 12). The paper offers no evidence that its own framework has been used in classrooms.
- There is a possible tension: the authors resist workforce framing (p. 3) but also present teaching non-use as "a basic component of workplace readiness" (p. 15).
- Text quirk: the PDF prints "usage" as "U.S.A.ge" (pp. 9, 15) and "refusal" as "RefU.S.A.l" (p. 23). This looks like a find-and-replace error. Quote with care.
