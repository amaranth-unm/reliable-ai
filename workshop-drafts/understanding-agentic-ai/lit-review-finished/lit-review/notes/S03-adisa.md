# S03-adisa

## Citation and scope

- Citation: Adisa, Kofi, Antonio Byrd, Estela Ene, et al. (MLA-CCCC Joint Task Force on Writing and AI). *Building a Culture for Generative AI Literacy in College Language, Literature, and Writing*. Working Paper 3. Modern Language Association of America, October 2024. Twenty listed authors, with Adisa first (p. 2). "Working Paper 3" comes from the PDF's mention of "our first two working papers" (p. 4); that label is not on the title page.
- PDF filename: pdfs/S03-adisa.pdf
- Pages read: 1-23, all of them. The PDF page numbers match the printed page numbers. Body text is pp. 4-21, the works cited are pp. 22-23.
- Kind of work: a working paper and framework. It sets out tiered recommendations and learning outcomes for students, educators, programs or departments, and institutions. The paper says it has not gone through CCCC or MLA approval and "does not necessarily reflect CCCC, NCTE, or MLA policy" (p. 2).

## The argument in its own terms

The task force argues that language, literature and writing educators should use their shared expertise to shape how generative AI (GAI) is used, both in their classrooms and in society. The reason given is that GAI is now built into everyday tools, and there are "no clear dividing lines between what is and isn't a GAI tool" (p. 5). The paper defines GAI literacy using the New London Group's multiliteracies framing. In that framing, GAI literacy is more than technical fluency: it includes critical, rhetorical and ethical judgment about when to use GAI and when not to (pp. 5, 21 n1). This literacy should be built at every level: students, educators, programs and departments, and institutions. The conclusion describes critical AI literacy as "neither for nor against the use of AI" (p. 21).

## Key ideas

1. **GAI is statistical, not a thinker, so humans must supervise it.** GAI systems "are not thinkers but statistical models that 'learn' by recognizing patterns," and their output can be "factually or demonstrably untrue" even when the grammar and syntax look plausible (p. 5). The paper says the term "artificial intelligence" itself creates unrealistic expectations (p. 5).
2. **Student learning outcomes for deciding whether and how to use GAI.** The paper lists outcomes (pp. 6-8). Students should be able to evaluate output for accuracy, relevance and bias. They should understand privacy, adapt as tools change, and analyze the rhetorical situation to decide on appropriate uses "(if any)". They should explain their choices (metacognition), disclose GAI use through attribution or meta-commentary, and know the policies that apply. They should also watch for when overreliance "prevents learning" or "limits their growth" as writers, and should prompt by drawing on existing rhetorical skills.
3. **Transparency, modeled by instructors.** Instructors should "model the ethic of transparency that they would want students to emulate" (p. 10). The authors also caution against using LLMs to assess student writing or give feedback, citing "the danger of undermining trust and human connection" (p. 10). For students, writers "will always err on the side of transparency" (p. 7).
4. **Equity, bias and the right to opt out.** GAI detectors and instructor suspicion fall unevenly on multilingual and international writers (p. 11). Instructors who choose to opt out should be respected, but the paper notes that opting out moves the burden of teaching to colleagues, who may be in more precarious positions (p. 11). Academic support units should not include "surveillance of and reporting on learners' GAI use" (p. 15).
5. **Build literacy at program and institution level, not as an add-on.** The paper recommends folding GAI literacy into existing outcomes rather than adding new ones, which avoids the "'add-on' approach" (p. 13). Curricula should be designed backward from the final course in a program, starting with first-year writing (p. 14). Faculty development should be ongoing, because "the one-shot workshop model will not support the intended outcomes" (p. 16). Institutions are told to treat AI research as happening across the humanities and not only in STEM. They should be cautious about unproven ed tech and about using AI in hiring and admissions, and they should keep human oversight (pp. 17-20).

## What the author says is new, and what it argues against

- **Claimed as new or urgent.** The paper presents GAI literacy as "called for, urgently" because GAI is now embedded in everyday software and cannot simply be avoided (p. 5). It adds a defined vocabulary that separates "AI literacy," "AI literacies" and "critical AI literacy" (p. 21 n1). It also extends literacy beyond students to "all agents in the academic enterprise" (p. 5), which it treats as its distinctive organizing move. It describes itself as building on two earlier working papers (p. 4).
- **Argued against:**
  - Acquiescence. The authors acknowledge the risk that writing such documents means "foreclosing alternatives by acquiescing rather than resisting" (p. 4).
  - Ignoring GAI, which it calls "not a sustainable position" (p. 9).
  - Treating AI ethics only as academic-dishonesty warnings (p. 17).
  - The "plagiarism narrative," set against attribution as professional practice (p. 13).
  - Over-reliance on GAI detectors (pp. 8, 11).
  - Add-on and one-shot approaches (pp. 13, 16).
  - The assumption that AI research belongs only to STEM or to R1 institutions (pp. 17-18).
  - Efficiency claims that override other institutional values (p. 20).
  - "Uncritical acceptance of AI technologies" that serves "neoliberal aims" (p. 21).

## For using AI

- Evaluate outputs for accuracy, relevance and bias. Check references through library databases, search engines or the print record (p. 6).
- Treat use as a judgment about the rhetorical situation. Break a task into steps and assess "at each phase what GAI has to offer" (p. 7).
- Disclose use through footnotes, author's notes, citations or "an appendix or link with the full record of prompts and outputs" (p. 7).
- Read the terms of service and ask what data the company collects (pp. 6-7).
- Prompt by drawing on rhetorical awareness, including chain-of-thought prompting and iterating (p. 8).
- Instructors should "experience and experiment" and "play with the tools" (p. 9). They should not use LLMs to grade or to write tailored feedback (p. 10).
- Institutions should keep humans in the loop for decisions such as hiring and admissions, and weigh efficiency against other values (pp. 19-20).
- Interpretation: the guidance assumes chatbot-style, single-exchange use (prompt, output, evaluate). It does not address multi-step or agentic tools that act on files or systems. The closest it comes is the remarks on GAI being embedded in everyday software (p. 5) and on "content drift," meaning output that cannot be reproduced (p. 9).

## For teaching AI

- The student learning outcomes (pp. 6-8) are written to be read by students and educators, and the paper mentions a separate student handout (p. 6).
- Lists of technical, pedagogical and ethical questions for educators (pp. 8-9).
- Syllabus and policy considerations, including that "scaffolding must occur in class" (pp. 11-12).
- Program-level outcomes, including "prompt engineering as a rhetorical practice" and attribution "beyond the plagiarism narrative" (p. 13).
- Backward course design and sequencing from developmental through upper-division courses (p. 14).
- Model course schedules, activities and syllabus language, including "modeling student agency to not use GAI" (p. 16).
- Ongoing faculty development (pp. 16-17).
- Guidance for writing centers and tutors (p. 15) and for graduate student instructors (p. 12).
- Literary and film criticism, especially of science fiction, is offered as a way to understand how popular attitudes to AI were formed (p. 19).

## Questions

- **Tension between opting out and providing literacy.** The paper respects the instructor's choice to opt out, but also says that choice shifts burdens to others and that GAI literacy is "vital" across curricula (pp. 11, 16). It does not say how to settle this tension within a department.
- **The paid-versus-free claim.** The paper says subscription versions "often" improve accuracy but that "price is not necessarily a measure of quality" (p. 6). This is left vague and cites no evidence.
- **How far its advice on AI-assisted assessment reaches.** It cautions against LLM grading and feedback (p. 10), but elsewhere says faculty "must use great care in incorporating AI technologies into the feedback and grading process" (p. 11). It is unclear whether this is a prohibition or a caution.
- **Where the line falls.** "Using GAI to facilitate learning" versus "a work-around or substitute for learning" is posed as a question (p. 9) and never operationalized.
- **How current it is.** The paper is a snapshot from early summer 2024 (p. 4), and its prompting advice is expected to date (p. 8). Interpretation: claims about what tools can and cannot do may already be out of date.
- **Recommendations without evidence of effect.** The program and institution recommendations are aspirational and rest on little empirical evidence of what works. The one empirical study it cites on policy, Chiu et al., is used to show that policy is incoherent (p. 15), not that any intervention succeeds.
- **The framing of "neoliberal aims" and "complicity."** These appear in the conclusion (p. 21) but are not developed in the body.
