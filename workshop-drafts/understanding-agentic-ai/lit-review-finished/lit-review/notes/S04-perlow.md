# S04-perlow

## Citation and scope

- Citation: Perlow, Seth. "Generative Theories, Pretrained Responses: Large AI Models and the Humanities." *PMLA*, vol. 139, no. 3, 2024, pp. 548-52. doi:10.1632/S0030812924000518. (Theories and Methodologies section, written in response to a prompt by Matthew Kirschenbaum and Rita Raley for a *PMLA* special feature.)
- PDF: pdfs/S04-perlow.pdf
- Pages read: PDF pp. 1-5 (all), which are journal pp. 548-552, including Works Cited.
- Kind of work: Short position essay or polemic in a forum.

## The argument in its own terms

Perlow argues that humanists' defensive first reactions to generative AI ("LLM verse lacks ... something") should be questioned. Large AI models change how the disciplines relate to each other. They make humanities discourse computable, while engineers have to deal with models in qualitative, rhetorical ways because the models resist technical interpretation. He says this leaves humanities theory, especially poststructuralist accounts of language and subjectivity, "uniquely equipped" for the moment. Politically, he argues that many early critiques repeat existing social thought (environment, labor) or are "manifestly reactionary" (copyright), and that the left should defend open sharing rather than intellectual property.

## Key ideas

1. **AI models trouble the line between the sciences and the humanities in both directions** (PDF p. 1-2). The models make poems and close readings computable, yet they "elude scientific understanding": their parameter matrices are too large to interpret directly. Researchers fall back on blunt methods such as ablation, which Perlow compares to the case of Phineas Gage. He quotes Matt Welsh: "Nobody understands how large AI models work" (p. 2).
2. **Prompt engineering is rhetoric** (PDF p. 2). "The new field of prompt engineering can be understood as the rhetorical study of how AI models respond to inputs." His examples are "Let's think step by step" and gibberish jailbreak strings. Even expert programmers now work through "the rhetoric of natural language" rather than "the mechanics of code." Tokenizers, in turn, mathematize language in counterintuitive ways that "seem not to cut language at the joints" (p. 2).
3. **Theory got there first** (PDF p. 3). Following Ted Underwood, he says LLMs give empirical proof of the poststructuralist thesis that language "actively determines the contours of the thinkable." The "greater mistake is not to see machine-written texts as expressive of consciousness but to see our own subjectivity as uninvolved with the mechanistic underpinnings of language."
4. **Quality over quantity: a return to aesthetics and value** (PDF p. 3). Coherent text of any length is now easy to produce, so "with the issue of quantity rendered moot, that of quality remains." Criticism should take up judgments of value again, but without "politically backward ideas about the human, the creative, the authentic." In teaching, ask advanced students "not for longer essays but for better ideas."
5. **Some political critiques are redundant and the copyright critique is reactionary** (PDF pp. 4-5). He says environmental and labor harms are real but "bear no special relation to large AI models per se." Citing Akhil Rao, he argues that per-use costs are trivial. Genuinely new harms do exist: bespoke propaganda, deepfake pornography and opaque algorithmic decisions. He calls copyright objections a "new copyright fundamentalism" (Kirschenbaum and Raley's phrase) and a reversal of the left's free-culture commitments. He asks how training differs "in principle" from a person reading books in a library (p. 5).

## What the author says is new, and what it argues against

- Claimed as new: Large models reverse the usual alignment of disciplines. Computer scientists now need qualitative, rhetorical methods, and language is shown to be predictable by means "obscure to our intuition" (pp. 1-3). He also presents LLMs as empirical confirmation of older theory, while noting that the theory came first (Underwood, p. 3). His own framing claim is that early responses are "either redundant with existing social thought or manifestly reactionary" (p. 1).
- Argues against:
  - humanists' defensive, essentialist claims that machine art lacks true creativity or emotion (p. 1);
  - Bender et al.'s "Stochastic Parrots" emphasis on energy costs and hidden labor, which he treats as distorting scale or not specific to AI (p. 4);
  - copyright-based objections and lawsuits against AI firms, including those by Martin and Picoult and the *Andersen v. Stability* training claims (pp. 4-5);
  - the view that aesthetics is inherently elitist or conservative (p. 3).

## For using AI

- Prompting as a rhetorical practice. Phrasing matters in ways that are often intuitive ("Let's think step by step") and sometimes bizarre (gibberish jailbreaks). Results come from observed model behavior, not from understanding the parameters (p. 2).
- Caution: these intuitive effects "imply (wrongly) that LLMs have mental states" (p. 2). Models also "fabricat[e] evidence and behave unpredictably" (p. 3).
- Welsh's KidSafe example shows that natural-language instructions can do tasks that are hard to specify as algorithms (p. 2).
- Interpretation: The essay gives a rationale for treating prompt-writing as a skill humanists already have. It offers no practical method or workflow.

## For teaching AI

- Assessment: LLMs should shift attention from the quantity of student writing to its quality. Defining "better" is "a genuine intellectual task" (p. 3).
- Conceptual material for teaching: tokenization as a counterintuitive way to mathematize language, model opacity and interpretability limits, and the theory of subjectivity and language (pp. 2-3).
- Legal and political issues: fair use, *Warhol v. Goldsmith*, the NYT v. OpenAI verbatim-reproduction claim, *Andersen v. Stability*, and the gap between new harms and familiar ones (pp. 4-5).
- Interpretation: The essay hints at a pedagogy (judging ideas rather than page counts) but does not say how to put it into practice. Its explicit teaching content is one paragraph.

## Questions

- He dismisses environmental costs using per-query and per-flight comparisons, while also conceding that "continuing growth of model size and usage will increase their effects" (p. 4). Does the scale argument hold as total use grows? This seems contested.
- The library analogy for training data (p. 5) concedes that "language models are not people" but argues the principle is the same. Is copying for training really equivalent to a person reading? This is contested.
- What would an aesthetics of value look like that avoids "politically backward ideas about the human, the creative, the authentic"? He calls for one but doesn't sketch it (p. 3).
- He says the humanities are "uniquely equipped" (p. 3) and that politically engaged scholars are "well equipped to respond" to new harms (p. 4). What concrete practices or roles follow is unclear.
- He urges people to "hack, pirate, and otherwise mess with the proprietary AI models however we can" (p. 5). The scope and ethics of this call are not developed.
- The claim that verbatim reproduction "will likely remain marginal" (p. 5) is an empirical prediction he does not support.
