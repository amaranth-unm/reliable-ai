This paper interrogates the discursive construction of expertise in early twentieth-century public health campaigns, arguing that the professionalization of sanitary science cannot be understood apart from the racialized logics that structured its field of intervention. Drawing on an extensive archive of municipal records, professional correspondence, and popular ephemera, it demonstrates that the category of the "unsanitary" operated as a technology of governance well beyond its ostensible epidemiological function. The paper contributes to ongoing conversations in the history of medicine, critical race studies, and science and technology studies, and suggests that existing accounts have underestimated the extent to which these dynamics persist in contemporary public health infrastructures.
