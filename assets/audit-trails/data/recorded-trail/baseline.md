These three essays form a single conversation. All are from a 2021 Ada Lovelace Institute series on what the arts and humanities contribute to AI ethics. Tasioulas's essay opens the series, and Vallor and Powell each respond to it.

## The three sources

**1. John Tasioulas, "The role of the arts and humanities in thinking about artificial intelligence (AI)," Ada Lovelace Institute, 14 June 2021.** [Link](https://www.adalovelaceinstitute.org/blog/role-arts-humanities-thinking-artificial-intelligence-ai/)

Tasioulas gives the humanities three jobs:
- **Showing that AI's path is chosen.** It is "not a matter of destiny" but a series of human decisions, and we should ask who makes them.
- **Keeping ethics central and broad.** He rejects a "diminished" ethics that amounts to voluntary self-regulation. He argues that Kate Crawford's call (in *Atlas of AI*) to focus on power instead of ethics still depends on ethical ideas like justice, equality and democracy.
- **Challenging the dominant approach**, which he calls data-driven, optimising and utilitarian. His main example is Stuart Russell's *Human Compatible*.

In its place he proposes "three Ps":
- **Pluralism:** many values conflict, so no single thing can be optimised.
- **Procedures:** how a decision is made matters, not just the result. AI criminal sentencing raises concerns that AI cancer diagnosis doesn't.
- **Participation:** drawing on Alasdair MacIntyre, a good life is active, which bears on automation and work.

He ends by arguing that the humanities themselves need to be more democratic.

**2. Shannon Vallor, "Mobilising the intellectual resources of the arts and humanities," Ada Lovelace Institute series, 2021.** I read the copy republished by [Edinburgh Innovations](https://edinburgh-innovations.ed.ac.uk/news/mobilising-the-intellectual-resources-of-the-arts-and-humanities), dated 4 August 2021.

Vallor agrees with Tasioulas and pushes his points further:
- **Intelligence includes judging what's worth wanting.** Drawing on Harry Frankfurt, she says real intelligence can "discover what is good to want." An efficient system without that, like Bostrom's "paperclip maximizer," isn't wise.
- **The idea that AI is inevitable is political.** It serves people who benefit from the status quo.
- **History matters.** Treating AI as unprecedented hides its links to colonial and repressive practices, such as facial recognition and predictive policing.
- **Imagination and care.** She calls the "technological imagination" sterile and turns to literature and the arts as sources of alternatives. She also argues for values of repair, maintenance and restoration.

**3. Alison Powell, "Otherwises, and the contribution of the arts and humanities to ethical AI," Ada Lovelace Institute, 13 July 2021.** [Link](https://www.adalovelaceinstitute.org/?p=12035)

Powell treats the humanities as a way to imagine alternatives, or "otherwises":
- **Loosening the "techno-systems frame,"** a habit of thinking in which optimisation is the central example.
- **Fiction as ethical imagination.** She uses Ursula K. Le Guin's *The Dispossessed* and her JUST AI network's commissions with science-fiction authors.
- **Who frameworks leave out.** She draws on Sylvia Wynter's essay "No Humans Involved" to show how frameworks can strip out responsibility and humanity.

She also offers the most direct revision of Tasioulas: reframe his "procedures" as "practices." She argues that procedures assume existing power relations and can be completed mechanically, while practices "come alive in the doing."

## How they relate

All three reject three ideas: that AI is inevitable, that AI ethics is about optimising, and that ethics is just company self-regulation. They differ in emphasis:
- **Tasioulas** is the most philosophical. He offers a framework (the three Ps) and a defence of ethics against critics who focus on power.
- **Vallor** keeps his framework and adds history, science and technology studies (STS), and a stronger account of power.
- **Powell** moves furthest from his framework. She emphasises fiction, situated practice and the people ethical frameworks leave out.

## What to read closely

**Start with Tasioulas.** The other two respond to him, and his three Ps are the most reusable idea in the set.

If you'll only read one more, **choose Vallor**. She covers the most ground, and her points about inevitability and history are easy to use in teaching or argument. Read **Powell** if your interest is in fiction, design, or how ethics works in practice. Her shift from procedures to practices is the sharpest critique within the series.
