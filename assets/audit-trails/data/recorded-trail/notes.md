## S01: Ali, Dick, Dillon, Jones, Penn & Staley (2023), "Histories of artificial intelligence: a genealogy of power," *BJHS Themes* 8: 1–18

**How much I read:** All 18 PDF pages, including footnotes, acknowledgements and the "Cite this article" line. In this file the PDF page numbers and the printed page numbers are the same.

### 1. Access
- **What I read:** The full article.
- **Version:** Published Cambridge University Press version, open access under CC BY-NC-SA 4.0 (licence on PDF p. 1; "Published online by Cambridge University Press" in each page footer).
- **Extraction:** I read the pages as images. The text and footnotes were legible, and I found no gaps.
- **Genre:** This is the **introduction to a themed issue**. Pages 9–17 summarise the issue's own articles, which I have not read. My notes on those articles come only from the introduction's summaries.
- **Unread linked material:**
  - The supplementary "preface" (PDF p. 18, at https://doi.org/10.1017/bjt.2023.15)
  - The issue's articles by Penn, Stark, Hamid, Babintseva, Kirtchik, Schirvar, Mendon-Plasek, Lysen, Law, Taylor, Moreschi, R. Powell, Sahoo, and Hagerty, Aranda & Jemio
  - All works cited in the footnotes

### 2. Argument, concepts and approach
**Argument.** The issue moves away from origin myths, namely that AI "was invented at Dartmouth in the summer of 1956, or by Alan Turing in 1950" (p. 1). Instead it places AI within "plural genealogies" and "deeper histories and broader geographies" (p. 1). The authors treat AI as "a diffuse set of technologies and systems of epistemic and political power" (p. 1). The core claim is that AI extends managerial power. AI belongs to a "Management Age" (1500s to today) more than to the "Information Age" (1950s to today), and "the Management Age conditions the Information Age" (p. 4).

**Useful concepts:**
- **Genealogy, in Foucault's sense.** This means tracing several interconnected histories rather than searching for origins (p. 2). The guiding question is not what AI's origins are but "what are the histories within which it makes sense to bracket a host of political, technical and epistemic systems under this umbrella?" (p. 2).
- **Four themes from the History of AI seminar** (pp. 2–4):
  - *hidden labour*: the human work that makes AI systems function
  - *encoded behaviour*: how AI systems discipline or frame the behaviour of the people who use them
  - *disingenuous rhetoric*: the gap between how AI is imagined and promoted and how it is actually used, and how the framing of "AI" helps create the thing it describes
  - *cognitive injustice*: how definitions of "intelligence" narrow knowledge, especially for marginalised groups
- **Management Age versus Information Age** (pp. 4–5).
- **Synchronic interdisciplinarity.** Several disciplines bring their own methods to one object (p. 2). The authors draw on Sarah Dillon and on Roland Barthes's "From work to text."
- **A shared toolkit.** Historians and AI researchers have both described intelligence with concepts such as "network" and "systems" (p. 6).

**Approach.** The introduction combines historiographical critique with a map of the issue's articles. It is organised into four sections (p. 9):
1. Colonial and race-science genealogies (pp. 9–11)
2. Creativity, economy and the human–machine distinction, in the US, UK and USSR (pp. 11–13)
3. Computer vision (pp. 13–16)
4. Biometric states and "social implications" discourse (pp. 16–17)

### 3. What work the source does
- **Kinds of work:**
  - *Conceptual and historiographical*: it reframes what "history of AI" is a history *of* (p. 1)
  - *Historical comparison*: the US and USSR, Cold War and non-Cold War settings, India, Argentina and Brazil (pp. 5, 8)
  - *Synthesis*: it pulls together other scholarship and the issue's articles
- **Evidence:**
  - Mostly other scholarship, cited in very dense footnotes (for example nn. 7, 12, 15, 23, 28, 30).
  - A few published primary texts, quoted or cited directly:
    - Ada Lovelace's notes on Menabrea (1842; n. 36, p. 11)
    - Evald Ilyenkov on "man–machine" (1966; quoted via Kirtchik, n. 37, p. 11)
    - J. C. R. Licklider's "Man–computer symbiosis" (1960; n. 38, p. 12)
    - The 1994 exchange between Diana Forsythe and James Fleck in *Social Studies of Science* (n. 17, p. 6)
  - Archival, image and interview evidence appears only second-hand, through the summaries of the issue's articles. One example is Moreschi's experiments with image datasets (p. 15).
- **Period of the evidence:** The issue claims a span from the sixteenth century to the 2020s (p. 4). The summarised articles concentrate on the 1920s–2020s, especially the Cold War (pp. 7–8).
- **Evaluation on its own terms:** As an introduction, it does what it sets out to do. It gives a clear organising question (p. 2), named themes and a periodisation. It also positions the issue against both practitioner histories and early social-science critiques (pp. 5–6).
- **Limits of the evidence:** The "Management Age" thesis is stated as a framing claim. Its support is the issue's articles, which are described here but not shown. So "the Management Age conditions the Information Age" (p. 4) is best read as a programme the issue proposes, not a result this introduction demonstrates.

### 4. Passages for closer reading
- **PDF pp. 1–2:** from "'AI' is everywhere and nowhere" to the guiding question ("not 'what are the origins of this thing called AI?' but rather…"). This is the clearest statement of the genealogical framing.
- **PDF pp. 4–5, "Historiographies of AI and management":** the Management Age and Information Age argument. It is the source's most distinctive historical claim.

### 5. The authors' claims, my interpretation, and an open question
- **The authors' claim:** AI is "less a history… than a genealogy" (p. 2). Histories of AI also already exist, "even if they are as yet unrecognized in their pertinence and multiplicity" (p. 2).
- **The authors' qualifications, kept as stated:**
  - Historians "do not occupy a privileged position" and share concepts with AI (p. 6).
  - On the Cold War: "Some of these touchpoints originated in the Cold War; others did not" (p. 7).
  - Lysen's radiology case is said to show that these technologies "have aided, without in any way escaping, the necessity of human judgement" (p. 15).
  - The aim is "not to critique AI for the purpose of its betterment" but to get a clearer picture of "what and where AI *is*, what and where it *might be*, and what and where it perhaps *should not be*" (p. 17).
- **The authors' claim about the uses of history:** They quote Lorraine Daston: history can "unsettle present certainties and thereby enlarge our sense of the thinkable" (p. 17).
- **My interpretation:** For a historical framing, the main contribution is a change in the question. Instead of asking where AI came from, it asks which longer histories make AI understandable. The answer the authors favour is management, control and coloniality, more than logic and computing.
- **An open question (mine):** The authors admit that "'AI' is everywhere and nowhere, and so is its history" (p. 1). If the genealogy runs back to the 1500s and through management in general, what still counts as a history *of AI* rather than a history of management? The introduction doesn't set boundaries for this. The individual articles may.

### 6. Explicit responses and citations
- **Explicit responses:**
  - It positions itself against origin myths that cite Dartmouth 1956 and Turing 1950 (p. 1).
  - It contrasts practitioner histories (Nilsson; Russell & Norvig; Newell; McCarthy; Boden) with critical social studies (Suchman, Forsythe, Collins, Agre, Dreyfus, Fleck) (pp. 5–6, n. 15).
  - It reports the 1994 Forsythe–Fleck exchange (p. 6).
- **Citations relevant to this source set:**
  - Cave, Dihal & Dillon (eds.), *AI Narratives* (2020) (n. 12, p. 4)
  - Kate Crawford, *Atlas of AI* (n. 7; n. 31)
  - Suchman, *Human–Machine Reconfigurations* (n. 15; p. 6)
  - Paul Edwards, *The Closed World* (n. 20)
  - I did **not** find a citation of S03 (Bory, Natale & Trudel 2021) in the footnotes.
- **Thematic comparison (our interpretation, not a stated relationship):** S01 and S03 both reject the Dartmouth-centred origin story and both criticise histories written by AI practitioners. S01 pushes AI's history toward management, labour and coloniality. S03 pushes it toward communication and media. Neither text responds to the other.

### 7. Recommended next reading action
- **What to read:** First reread **PDF pp. 1–2 and 4–5** closely. Then look for **Sarah T. Hamid's "History as capture"** in the same issue. The introduction summarises it on **PDF pp. 10–11**.
- **Why:** You're interested in historical framing, and Hamid's article, as summarised, turns AI critique back onto historical scholarship itself. It questions whether history can "and should" be used to critique "the cultural hegemony" of computing, and asks readers to write history "differently" (pp. 10–11). That bears directly on what historical framing can and can't do.
- **Access basis:** The pp. 1–5 recommendation rests on the full text. The Hamid recommendation is **provisional**: it rests only on the introduction's summary. I haven't opened Hamid's article or confirmed whether it is open access.

## S03: Bory, Natale & Trudel (2021), "Artificial Intelligence: Reframing Thinking Machines Within the History of Media and Communication," De Gruyter

**How much I read:** All of the text on PDF pp. 1–19 (printed pp. 95–113), including the references. PDF p. 20 is blank. The printed page number equals the PDF page number plus 94.

### 1. Access
- **What I read:** The full chapter.
- **Version:** Published De Gruyter version, open access under CC BY-NC-ND 4.0 (PDF p. 1). It carries DOI 10.1515/9783110740202-006 and the publisher's page layout.
- **Extraction:** I read the pages as images. The text was legible.
- **Missing details:** The PDF does **not** give the title or editors of the volume it appears in, so I still can't complete the citation in sources.md from this file. It does confirm the page range: pp. 95–113.
- **Unread linked material:** All cited works, including Natale 2021 (*Deceitful Media*), Bory 2019, and Cave, Dihal & Dillon 2020.

### 2. Argument, concepts and approach
**Argument.** Canonical histories of AI are teleological "great minds" stories, written largely by AI practitioners (PDF p. 3 [97]). The authors propose media and communication history as "one of the possible standpoints" for writing "histories" of AI (PDF p. 4 [98]). They develop this through four "aspects," or as they say in the conclusion, "trajectories" (PDF p. 1 [95]; PDF p. 14 [108]):
1. **Cybernetics and communication theory.** AI was partly defined by separating itself from cybernetics (PDF pp. 4–7 [98–101]).
2. **The Turing Test as a "Communication Game."** The split between AI and HCI is "the fruit of a retrospective partition" (PDF pp. 7–9 [101–103]).
3. **Science fiction and media narratives.** These have shaped AI research, design and promotion (PDF pp. 10–12 [104–106]).
4. **Games.** Games serve as testbeds and public showcases for communication between humans and machines (PDF pp. 12–13 [106–107]).

**Useful concepts:**
- **"Pantheonization."** A teleological, chronological approach to the history of AI as a history of progress by great minds (PDF p. 3 [97]).
- **Historiography of AI as "translation."** Following Ekbia, writing AI's history is partly "a translation process aimed at people outside the field." Such narratives "legitimize specific actors and technologies" (PDF p. 3 [97]).
- **The "Communication Game."** A reading of the Turing Test, after Natale 2021: the test measures human perception rather than machine cognition (PDF p. 8 [102]).
- **Meta-communication.** Play as communication about communication, after Gregory Bateson (PDF p. 12 [106]).
- **"Specific forms of AI before AI."** All media can be read this way (PDF p. 14 [108]).

**Approach.** A historiographical critique followed by a thematic survey, written from within media and communication studies.

### 3. What work the source does
- **Kinds of work:**
  - *Historiographical critique* (Section 1)
  - *Historical reinterpretation* of known episodes, such as Dartmouth's break from cybernetics and the Turing Test
  - *Cultural and media interpretation* of fiction, film and games
- **Evidence:** Published texts and other scholarship. I saw no archival documents or interviews, though McCorduck's 1979 interviews appear second-hand.
  - Primary published texts:
    - Wiener (1948)
    - Shannon (1949, 1950, 1953)
    - Turing (1947 lecture; 1950; 1953)
    - Minsky (1961), quoted at length on PDF p. 9 [103]
    - Samuel Butler, "Darwin among the Machines" (1863), quoted on PDF p. 11 [105]
  - McCarthy quotations taken from Nilsson (2010) and McCorduck (1979) (PDF pp. 6–7 [100–101])
  - Films:
    - *Metropolis* (1927)
    - *2001: A Space Odyssey* (1968)
    - *Blade Runner* (1982)
    - *Short Circuit* (1986)
    - *Her* (2013)
  - Devices and public events: the eighteenth-century Mechanical Turk, Deep Blue, AlphaGo, Watson, and the robot Sophia
- **Period of the evidence:** Concentrated in the 1940s–1960s, with examples from antiquity (Byzantine lamps, Homer and Ovid via Nilsson) through the 2010s.
- **Evaluation on its own terms:** The chapter aims to show that media and communication history is a productive vantage point. It succeeds most clearly on cybernetics and the Turing Test, where it uses primary texts.
- **Where the evidence is thinner:** The science-fiction section relies mostly on examples. The claim that fictional narratives "have deeply influenced the development of real AI" (PDF p. 12 [106]) is supported by illustrative cases and by one study of science-fiction references in HCI literature (Jordan et al. 2018; PDF p. 11 [105]). It covers four large areas briefly, which fits a survey chapter.

### 4. Passages for closer reading
- **PDF pp. 3–4 [97–98]:** from "Such a pantheonization testifies…" to "…histories – of AI." This is the historiographical argument and the authors' modest positioning.
- **PDF pp. 15–16 [109–110]:** the conclusion paragraph beginning "From a historical perspective, moreover, the focus on communication works as an invitation to rewrite not only the history of AI but also its prehistory." It ends with the claim that historicising communication "challenges the idea that AI is unprecedented."

### 5. The authors' claims, my interpretation, and an open question
- **The authors' claims:**
  - The relationship between communication and AI "is neither a novelty nor a recent evolution" (PDF p. 2 [96]).
  - AI is "a field so haunted by cybernetics that it often prefers to ignore its old demons" (PDF p. 7 [101]).
  - Placing AI within communication history "challenges the idea that AI is unprecedented" (PDF p. 15 [109]).
- **The authors' qualifications, kept as stated:**
  - "Rather than proposing a History of AI, we more modestly propose…" (PDF p. 4 [98]).
  - "while AI was not invented before the 1950s, it had many precursors" (PDF p. 2 [96]).
  - The etymology of intelligence "*may* come from" *inter* + *legere* (PDF p. 14 [108]).
- **My interpretation:** For historical framing, the chapter offers a second way out of the Dartmouth story. It changes the lineage, placing AI with telegraphy, the typewriter, media and play rather than with logic. That makes AI's present uses as a conversational medium, such as chatbots and voice assistants, look like a continuation rather than a rupture. Power and political economy appear only briefly, for example in military funding and in companies using games to present their AI (PDF pp. 6–7, 13 [100–101, 107]).
- **An open question (mine):** If "all media… can… be seen as… specific forms of AI before AI" (PDF p. 14 [108]), does the category of AI become too broad to be historically useful? This is the same boundary problem S01 raises with "everywhere and nowhere," but here it comes from widening the scope to media rather than to management.

### 6. Explicit responses and citations
- **Explicit responses:**
  - It critiques practitioner and founder histories: McCorduck, Crevier and Nilsson. It also uses Newell's own 1983 warning that the participant-observer's account "is at least tinged with bias, if not steeped in it" (PDF p. 3 [97]).
  - Footnote 1 (PDF p. 4 [98]) lists earlier critiques of the canonical narrative: Dreyfus, Collins and Edwards.
  - It explicitly builds on Ekbia (2008), Gunkel (2020), Guzman & Lewis (2019) and Natale (2021).
- **Citations relevant to this source set:**
  - Cave, Dihal & Dillon (eds.), *AI Narratives* (2020) (PDF p. 10 [104]), plus Truitt's chapter in it (PDF p. 3 [97])
  - Suchman (2007) (PDF p. 9 [103])
  - Edwards (1997) (PDF p. 4 [98]; PDF p. 6 [100])
- **Cited by both S01 and S03 (a factual overlap only):** Cave/Dihal/Dillon, Suchman, Edwards, Collins, Nilsson, Newell (2000) and Licklider (1960).
- **Thematic comparison:** See the S01 note. This is our interpretation; neither text responds to the other.

### 7. Recommended next reading action
- **What to read:** Reread **PDF pp. 3–4 [97–98]** alongside **S01 PDF pp. 5–6**.
- **Why:** You're interested in historical framing, and both passages criticise histories of AI written by practitioners. Reading them together shows two different alternative lineages, communication and media versus management and power, built from a shared critique. Then, if you want the communication argument at full length, the chapter points to **Natale 2021, *Deceitful Media*** for the "Communication Game" reading (PDF p. 8 [102]).
- **Access basis:** The rereading recommendation rests on the full text of both PDFs. The Natale suggestion is **provisional**: I haven't opened it or checked whether it is open access.
