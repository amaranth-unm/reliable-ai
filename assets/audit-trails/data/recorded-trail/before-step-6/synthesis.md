# Synthesis: historical framing of AI

**Question:** How do arts and humanities perspectives help us think about AI?
**Interest:** Historical framing (sources.md)
**Based on:** readings/S01-ali.pdf (all pages read), readings/S03-bory.pdf (all pages read), notes.md, sources.md
**Date:** 2026-11-11
**Status:** Draft for checking.

## Limits of this set
- **Only two sources have been read.** S02 (Elam) is cited in sources.md, but no PDF was provided, so nothing below draws on its content.
- **Both read sources are framing pieces rather than case studies.** S01 is the introduction to a themed issue, and many of its claims rest on the issue's articles, which I know only from S01's summaries (S01 pp. 9–17). S03 is a survey chapter covering four areas briefly.
- **Coverage is narrow.** Both were published in 2021–2023 and come from Anglophone and European history of science and media studies. Neither is a dialogue. Creative practice appears only second-hand: S03's readings of fiction and film, and S01's summary of Moreschi (S01 p. 15).
- **Generative AI barely appears.** S01 mentions ChatGPT (pp. 1, 13). S03 predates it.
- These limits mean the synthesis maps **two historical framings**, not the field.

Page numbers are PDF pages. For S03, the printed page number follows in brackets; printed = PDF + 94.

## The shared starting point (explicit in both)
Both sources reject a canonical origin story centred on Dartmouth, and both criticise histories written by AI practitioners.
- **S01** moves "beyond conventional origin myths" of Dartmouth 1956 and Turing 1950 (p. 1). It contrasts practitioner histories with early critical social studies (pp. 5–6, n. 15).
- **S03** criticises "pantheonization" and a "teleological and chronological approach" (PDF p. 3 [97]). It notes that the first histories were written largely by people in the field. It quotes Newell's 1983 warning that the participant-observer's account "is at least tinged with bias, if not steeped in it" (PDF p. 3 [97]).

These are parallel responses to the same historiography. Neither source responds to the other (see notes.md, S01 §6 and S03 §6).

## What each source adds

### S01: AI as part of a genealogy of managerial power
- **Method: genealogy, in Foucault's sense.** S01 traces several intertwined histories rather than looking for an origin. Its guiding question asks which histories make it "make sense to bracket a host of political, technical and epistemic systems under this umbrella" (p. 2).
- **Periodisation: a "Management Age" (1500s to today).** This, the authors say, "conditions" the "Information Age" (1950s to today) (p. 4).
- **Analytic themes:** hidden labour, encoded behaviour, disingenuous rhetoric and cognitive injustice (pp. 2–4).
- **Geography and plurality.** S01 compares the US and the USSR (pp. 11–13), and covers India and Argentina (pp. 16–17). It asks "which Cold War?" rather than treating the Cold War as a single context (p. 7).
- **The historian's position.** Historians "do not occupy a privileged position." They share concepts such as "network" and "systems" with AI (p. 6).
- **Purpose of history.** The aim is "not to critique AI for the purpose of its betterment." Instead it is to clarify what AI is, might be and "should not be." S01 links this to a "politics of refusal" (pp. 17–18).

### S03: AI as part of media and communication history
- **Standpoint: media and communication history.** S03 offers this as "one of the possible standpoints" for writing "histories" of AI. It explicitly says it is "more modestly" not proposing "a History of AI" (PDF p. 4 [98]).
- **Lineage: cybernetics, telegraphy and the typewriter, fiction and film, play.** S03 argues that the name "artificial intelligence" was chosen partly "to escape the association with cybernetics" (McCarthy, quoted via Nilsson; PDF p. 6 [100]).
- **Reinterpretation: the Turing Test as a "Communication Game."** On this reading the test measures human perception rather than machine cognition (PDF p. 8 [102]). The separation of AI from HCI is "the fruit of a retrospective partition" (PDF p. 9 [103]).
- **Narratives and games shape AI.** S03 argues that fiction and film have shaped AI research, design and promotion (PDF pp. 10–12 [104–106]), and that games have served as testbeds and public showcases (PDF pp. 12–13 [106–107]).
- **Purpose of history.** Historicising communication "challenges the idea that AI is unprecedented" (PDF p. 15 [109]).

## My interpretations (labelled)
1. **Both treat continuity as a tool, but use it for different ends.** In S01, continuity shows managerial power growing and becoming naturalised (p. 18). In S03, continuity shows that AI is not unprecedented as a medium (PDF p. 15 [109]). I don't read this as disagreement: neither source denies the other's lineage.
2. **Both treat the AI/HCI boundary as historically contingent, from different angles.** S03 calls the split a retrospective partition (PDF p. 9 [103]). S01 reports that what counted as AI in the USSR in the 1970s "came to be branded primarily as 'human–computer interaction' in the United States" (p. 12). This is an inferred convergence. Neither source makes the link.
3. **Both say that how AI is narrated helps make AI.** S01 says the rhetorical framing of "AI" "performatively creates and shapes the very phenomenon" (p. 4). S03 says writing AI's history is partly a "translation" that legitimises actors (PDF p. 3 [97]), and that fiction shapes design and promotion (PDF p. 12 [106]).
4. **Both face a boundary problem, coming from opposite expansions.** S01 widens AI's history toward management. S03 widens it toward all media ("AI before AI," PDF p. 14 [108]). S01 acknowledges the issue ("everywhere and nowhere," p. 1). Neither sets explicit limits. This is my question, not a criticism either source makes.

## What this set suggests for the reading question (provisional)
From these two sources, arts and humanities perspectives help with AI by changing **which past AI is compared with**. That choice decides what looks new and what looks continuous, and it can unsettle "present certainties" (Daston, quoted in S01 p. 17). Two read sources aren't enough to generalise beyond this.
