# Synthesis: historical framing of AI

**Question:** How do arts and humanities perspectives help us think about AI?
**Interest:** Historical framing (sources.md)
**Based on:** readings/S01-ali.pdf (all pages read), readings/S03-bory.pdf (all pages read), notes.md, sources.md
**Date:** 2026-11-11
**Revised:** 2026-11-11 following decisions.md
**Status:** Draft for checking.

## Limits of this set
- **Only two sources have been read.** S02 (Elam) is cited in sources.md, but no PDF was provided, so nothing below draws on its content.
- **Both read sources are framing pieces rather than case studies.** S01 is the introduction to a themed issue, and many of its claims rest on the issue's articles, which I know only from S01's summaries (S01 pp. 9–17). S03 is a survey chapter covering four areas briefly.
- **Coverage is narrow.** Both were published in 2021–2023 and come from Anglophone and European history of science and media studies. Neither is a dialogue. Creative practice appears only second-hand: S03's readings of fiction and film, and S01's summary of Moreschi (S01 p. 15).
- **Generative AI barely appears.** S01 mentions ChatGPT (pp. 1, 13). S03 predates it.
- These limits mean the synthesis maps **two historical framings**, not the field.

Page numbers are PDF pages. For S03, the printed page number follows in brackets; printed = PDF + 94.

## The shared starting point: critique of the canonical narrative (explicit in both, with different targets)
Both sources criticise the canonical narrative of AI's history, but they aim at different things. S01 rejects origin stories as such. S03 accepts that AI emerged in the 1950s and criticises the "pantheonization" and teleology of how that emergence is told.
- **S01** moves "beyond conventional origin myths" of Dartmouth 1956 and Turing 1950 (p. 1). It lists practitioner histories alongside critical social studies (p. 5, n. 15) without criticising them. Its remark that "Many focused on the small (yet assertive) elite Western academic communities" (p. 5) may be a mild criticism. It is unclear whether "many" refers to practitioner histories or to all early histories.
- **S03** criticises "pantheonization" and a "teleological and chronological approach" (PDF p. 3 [97]). It notes that the first histories were written largely by people in the field. It quotes Newell's 1983 warning that "the accuracy of the participant observer is at least tinged with bias, if not steeped in it" (PDF p. 3 [97]). It keeps the 1950s as the point of emergence: "while AI was not invented before the 1950s, it had many precursors" (PDF p. 2 [96]). It describes the canonical narrative as centred on three meetings (1955, 1956, 1958) and on "founding fathers" (PDF pp. 3–4 [97–98]).

These are parallel but differently aimed responses to the canonical narrative. Neither source responds to the other (see notes.md, S01 §6 and S03 §6).

## What each source adds

### S01: AI as part of a genealogy of managerial power
- **Method: genealogy, in Foucault's sense.** S01 traces several intertwined histories rather than looking for an origin. Its guiding question is "what are the histories within which it makes sense to bracket a host of political, technical and epistemic systems under this umbrella?" (p. 2).
- **Periodisation: a "Management Age" (1500s to today).** This, the authors say, "conditions" the "Information Age" (1950s to today) (p. 4).
- **Analytic themes:** hidden labour, encoded behaviour, disingenuous rhetoric and cognitive injustice (pp. 2–4).
- **Geography and plurality.** S01 summarises the issue's articles that compare the US, the UK and the USSR (pp. 9, 11–13) and that cover India and Argentina (pp. 16–17). The comparisons are made in those articles, which I have not read. S01 itself asks "which Cold War?" rather than treating the Cold War as a single context (p. 7).
- **The historian's position.** Historians "do not occupy a privileged position." They share concepts such as "network" and "systems" with AI (p. 6).
- **Purpose of history.** The aim is "not to critique AI for the purpose of its betterment." Instead it is to develop a clearer picture of what AI is, might be and "perhaps *should not be*." S01 places this aim next to a "growing politics of refusal" (p. 17).

### S03: AI as part of media and communication history
- **Standpoint: media and communication history.** S03 offers this as "one of the possible standpoints" for writing "histories" of AI. It explicitly says it is "more modestly" not proposing "a History of AI" (PDF p. 4 [98]).
- **Lineage: cybernetics, telegraphy and the typewriter, fiction and film, play.** S03 argues that the name "artificial intelligence" was chosen partly "to escape the association with cybernetics" (McCarthy, quoted via Nilsson; PDF p. 6 [100]).
- **Reinterpretation: the Turing Test as a "Communication Game."** On this reading the test measures human perception rather than machine cognition (PDF p. 8 [102]). The separation of AI from HCI is "the fruit of a retrospective partition" (PDF p. 9 [103]).
- **Narratives and games shape AI.** S03 argues that fiction and film have shaped AI research, design and promotion (PDF pp. 10–12 [104–106]), and that games have served as testbeds and public showcases (PDF pp. 12–13 [106–107]).
- **Purpose of history.** Historicising communication "challenges the idea that AI is unprecedented" (PDF p. 15 [109]).

## My interpretations (labelled)
1. **Both treat continuity as a tool, but use it for different ends.** In S01, continuity shows managerial power growing and becoming naturalised (p. 18). In S03, continuity shows that AI is not unprecedented as a medium (PDF p. 15 [109]). I don't read this as disagreement: neither source denies the other's lineage.
2. **Both treat the AI/HCI boundary as historically contingent, from different angles.** S03 says the separation of AI and HCI was a "disciplinary distinction that emerged in the 1970s and 1980s but was also applied retrospectively" (PDF p. 7 [101]), and calls the split a retrospective partition (PDF p. 9 [103]). S01 reports that what counted as AI in the USSR in the 1970s "came to be branded primarily as 'human–computer interaction' in the United States" (p. 12). This is an inferred convergence. Neither source makes the link. Two qualifications apply. First, the contingencies differ: S01's is cross-national and synchronic (USSR vs US), while S03's is retrospective and diachronic. Second, S01's claim summarises Kirtchik's and Schirvar's articles, which I have not read.
3. **Both say that how AI is narrated helps make AI.** S01 says the rhetorical framing of "AI" "performatively creates and shapes the very phenomenon" (p. 4). S03, adopting Ekbia's term, says the historiography of AI is "at least partly a translation process" whose narratives legitimise specific actors (PDF p. 3 [97]). It also says fiction shapes design and promotion (PDF p. 12 [106]).
4. **Both widen AI's history in different directions, which raises a boundary question of my own.** S01 widens it toward management. Its "everywhere and nowhere" (p. 1) introduces an argument for widening AI's history, not an acknowledgement of a boundary problem. S03 widens it toward all media ("AI before AI," PDF p. 14 [108]). But it marks a limit: "AI was not invented before the 1950s" (PDF p. 2 [96]), and the phrase "AI *before* AI" keeps media and AI distinct. S01's introduction states no such limit. How far AI's history can extend before it stops being a history of AI is my question. Neither source raises it.

## What this set suggests for the reading question (provisional)
From these two sources, arts and humanities perspectives help with AI by changing **which past AI is compared with**. That choice decides what looks new and what looks continuous, and it can unsettle "present certainties" (Daston, quoted in S01 p. 17). Two read sources aren't enough to generalise beyond this.
