# Synthesis: how arts and humanities perspectives help us think about AI

Question and interest: see [brief.md](brief.md). This is based on the three source notes ([S01](notes/S01-hofman.md), [S02](notes/S02-chiodo.md), [S03](notes/S03-raley.md)), each written from a full read of its PDF. Page numbers are those used in the notes: S01 PDF pages; S02 and S03 printed pages. Three sources are a small sample, so this is an orientation, not a survey.

## Three different kinds of work

The three sources do not answer the same question, and they are not in dialogue. None cites another (S01 §6, S02 §6, S03 §6 in the notes).

- **S01 Hofman** treats the humanities as a resource of *ethical theory* for practitioners. Six ethical traditions become "lenses" for designers using generative AI inside the double-diamond process (S01, pp. 5–8). It also offers a practical labelling scheme to move part of the "ethical burden" upstream (S01, p. 4). Creative practice here is commercial design work, and the humanities supply the questions a team asks of itself.
- **S02 Chiodo** treats the humanities as *philosophy of art* that can draw a line. Autonomous AI artefacts cannot be art, because art needs human "meta-sensemaking" (S02, p. 2). She argues this through thought experiments on Poe, Borges and Picasso (S02, pp. 5–9) and the Greek etymology of "automation" (S02, p. 6). Creative practice here is the history of Western art and literature, used as a standard.
- **S03 Raley and Rhee** treat the humanities as *critical, historical and interpretive practice*. "Critical AI … is itself a historical and epistemic formation" (S03, p. 188). Artworks such as Ridler's *Myriad (Tulips)* and Dinkins's *N'TOO* are themselves models of critique (S03, pp. 185–186, 191). Creative practice here is art that works with machine learning in order to expose it.

## What each adds on the interest

**Creative practice.**
- S01 adds a practitioner's view: ethics as part of the creative process, not a brake on it. It calls for "playful exploration" and "constraints as enablers" (S01, p. 9).
- S02 adds a criterion for when creative practice is art at all: the human process and its reasons, not the output's look (S02, pp. 2–5). It explicitly sets aside artists who use AI as one tool among others (S02, p. 2).
- S03 adds an account of creative practice *as* humanities research. Artists who work with ML "are particularly well positioned to stress test, evaluate, and exploit" it (S03, p. 191). It also adds the qualification that "not all so-termed AI art does this political and aesthetic work" (S03, n. 20, p. 200).

**Ethical concepts.**
- S01 works with named ethical theories and stakeholder-agreed labels (S01, pp. 4–6).
- S02 works with Kantian *autonomy* as both "opportunity" and "burden" (S02, p. 6), and with a proposed "right to always know the authorship of the artefacts" (S02, p. 10).
- S03 names its commitments as "ethicopolitical investments" (S03, p. 192) and frames ethics through political economy, labour, discrimination and data sovereignty (S03, pp. 189, 191), not through ethical theories.

**Historical framing.**
- S01 has little historical framing. Its theories are introduced by founders and contemporary proponents (S01, pp. 5–6).
- S02 frames the issue across very long durations: Greek etymology, "millennia" of art defined by shaping, and the move from classical to romantic culture (S02, pp. 3, 6, 9).
- S03 makes history a requirement: AI "cannot simply be thought in terms of the present and an irrationally exuberant future" (S03, p. 192). Ridler's tulips, which recall the Dutch tulip trade, "run counter to the still pervasive presentism" (S03, p. 186). Halpern's statement roots AI in Hayek's 1945 theory of knowledge (S03, pp. 195–196).

## Differences worth keeping

- **On burden** (my interpretation; see [burden and responsibility](ideas/burden-and-responsibility.md)). S01 aims to lighten the designer's burden, through labels (p. 4) and by not "over-burdening" creativity (p. 1). S02 reads relief from the burden of autonomy as what AI art offers us, and as the reason it is valued. She calls the wish "understandable, and even touching" but argues it "cannot succeed" (S02, p. 10). The two authors are not arguing with each other. They use the word in different registers: professional workload in S01, existential self-determination in S02.
- **On the human element in AI art.** S02 and S03 both cite Anna Ridler, which is a factual overlap. S02 quotes Ridler on "art without intent," or "wallpaper," and her interest in "reintroducing the human element" (S02, p. 5). It uses her to support the claim that the human makes the art. S03 values Ridler's authorial closeness to a data set she "collected and labeled herself," and the historical critique her work performs (S03, p. 186). The two agree on the importance of the human hand but differ on what it does: confer art status (S02) or open the black box (S03). This comparison is my interpretation.
- **On autonomy.** S02 sets autonomy against automation (p. 6). In S03's appendix, Bassett calls for "questions concerning autonomy (which don't reduce to automation)" (p. 194), and Parisi says AI "menaces the autonomy of the humanities" (p. 197). The phrasing is close, but Bassett and Parisi are distinct voices with their own aims, and there is no citation relation with S02. Parisi's critique of the "Promethean promise" of self-determination (S03, p. 197) may sit in tension with S02's defence of human autonomy as the condition of art. That is my reading, and it would need Parisi's fuller work to test.
- **On whether AI art is art.** S02 argues that "greatly autonomous" outputs are not art (p. 2). S03 does not take up that question. It treats artworks made with ML as critical objects (p. 186). S01 does not address art status at all.

## Qualifications to carry forward

- S01 reports no empirical test, only an "upcoming experiment" (p. 4). Its theory summaries are brief.
- S02's conclusion applies only to outputs "greatly autonomous from humans' inputs" (p. 2). It allows for unconscious sensemaking (pp. 4, 6).
- S03 is an introduction to a special issue. Many of its claims point to essays not read here, and its appendix voices are separate statements, not a shared position.

## Interpretation (labelled)

Taken together, the sources suggest three roles for the arts and humanities in thinking about AI: (1) a *toolbox* of ethical questions for practitioners (S01); (2) a *criterion* drawn from the history of art for what counts as art and why (S02); (3) a *critical practice*, scholarly and artistic, that historicises AI and opens it up (S03). This three-part frame is my own and is not stated by any source.
