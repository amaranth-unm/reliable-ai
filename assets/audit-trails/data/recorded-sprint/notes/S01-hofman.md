## 2. Argument, concepts, approach

**Author's claim.** AI ethics has focused on developers and given less attention to "technology consumers" such as professional designers who "mainly use AI in prepackaged configurable tools" (p. 2). Hofman proposes a framework, "Use, think, try," that sits inside the Design Council's double diamond model (pp. 2–3, 9):

- **Use (labelling).** Designers work mostly at the "consumption stage" of the AI lifecycle, so much "upstream governance and ethical considerations" lands on them (p. 4). Agreed labels, on the model of Nutri-Score and Eco-score, could "relieve the professional end user of part of the ethical burden" (p. 4). Hofman sketches an A–E openness label built from the Foundation Model Transparency Index (pp. 4–5) and a proposed sustainability label (p. 5).
- **Think (lenses).** Six ethical theories serve as "lenses": virtue, deontological, consequentialist, contract, care and existential ethics. Each is paired with a contemporary proponent: Vallor, Wright, Bostrom, Risse, Dignum and Terzis (pp. 5–6). The ethics is applied by "zooming out" (the macro level, at the big transitions of the double diamond) and "zooming in" (the micro and meso levels, at any time), with a table of example questions for three kinds of firm (pp. 6–8).
- **Try (play).** "Playful exploration" treats AI's flaws as "a kind of mirror of ourselves" (p. 10, drawing on Vallor) and AI as "playmates" rather than replacements (p. 10, drawing on Reigeluth).

**Concepts:** the ethical "conductor" (p. 4: a team member who guides others "in the sense of an orchestral conductor"); labels; ethical lenses; zooming in and out; anticipation and reflection (p. 9); "constraints as enablers" (p. 9, citing Rosso).

**Approach:** a conceptual and practical framework proposal for design practice, aimed at SMEs in creative industries (p. 4). It mentions "an upcoming experiment" with a transparency label (p. 4) but reports no empirical results. It summarises the ethical theories briefly, through secondary or contemporary figures, rather than analysing them in depth.

## 3. The work the source does

Applied ethics turned into a design method. Creative practice here means commercial design: creative agencies, product design and marketing. On its own terms the paper succeeds as a usable toolkit. Its stated aim is to avoid "over-burdening or compromising the creative process" (p. 1). It does not try to give historical or philosophical depth.

## 4. Passages for closer reading

- **pp. 6–7, "Zooming in and out."** Hofman says the model "was inspired by" Luke Munn's "The uselessness of AI ethics," which he summarises as arguing that AI ethics principles are "not only useless but even a dangerous distraction" (pp. 6–7). He then builds an ethics framework anyway. How he uses Munn is worth checking against Munn.
- **p. 10, "Playful exploration (try)."** AI is "a multi-faceted mirror reflecting our own good and bad traits." This is the paper's most humanistic claim about creative practice.

## 5. Claim, interpretation, open question

- **Author's claim:** using ethical theories as lenses within the design process makes solutions "more ethical" and also "richer and more diverse." It makes ethics "part of the normal design process toolkit" rather than "an afterthought burdening creativity" (p. 6).
- **Qualifications the author keeps:** the six theories "are certainly not the only possibilities" and overlap (p. 5). The questions in Table 1 are "not a final fixed list" and "should never lead to a definitive 'yes/no'" answer (pp. 8–9). Labels must be agreed by many stakeholders (p. 4). "There are no easy answers" (p. 10).
- **My interpretation:** "burden" is a working concept here. Labels shift part of the designer's ethical burden upstream (p. 4), and the framework is meant not to burden creativity (pp. 1, 6). Hofman treats lightening the designer's burden as a good. This is my reading, not the author's framing as a theme.
- **Open question:** if labels relieve end users of ethical burden, who carries the judgement that the labels encode, and does a label change the designer's own responsibility or only what they know?
