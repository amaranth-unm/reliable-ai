## 2. Argument, concepts, approach

This is a co-authored editors' introduction followed by an appendix of signed statements by six other scholars. The introduction's voice (Raley and Rhee) is kept separate from each appendix contributor below.

**Raley and Rhee's claims:**

- **Artworks as critical practice (pp. 185–186).** Anna Ridler's *Myriad (Tulips)* (2018) and *Mosaic Virus* "make visible the end-to-end apparatus of artificial intelligence," from human labelling to infrastructure (p. 185). By invoking "the Dutch tulip trade and the history of capital," Ridler's work "runs counter to the still pervasive presentism of the discourse on AI" (p. 186). Her self-labelled data set contrasts with ImageNet's "anonymous Mechanical Turkers" (p. 186).
- **AI versus ML (pp. 187–188).** "AI" is a hyped shorthand that "falsely implies something singular and unprecedented" (quoting Suchman, p. 187). Critical AI nonetheless uses the term pragmatically, "as concept, ideology, and *dispositif*" (p. 188).
- **What critical AI is (pp. 188–189).** "Criticality" means "working across disciplines," often in "proximity to the thing itself" (p. 188). "Critical AI, then, is itself a historical and epistemic formation" (p. 188). It surveys themes: correlationism, discrimination, labour and infrastructure (p. 189).
- **Language models and authorship (p. 190).** With GPT-3, "the determination of linguistic property and, by extension, responsibility is essentially foreclosed" (p. 190). Attribution becomes a research question, sharpened by the GPT-4chan example.
- **Dinkins (p. 191).** Stephanie Dinkins's *Not the Only One* (*N'TOO*), trained on "a small data set comprising oral histories provided by Dinkins, her aunt, and her niece," models "data sovereignty." Artists "are particularly well positioned to stress test, evaluate, and exploit" ML systems (p. 191). Note 20 qualifies this: "not all so-termed AI art does this political and aesthetic work" (p. 200).
- **Literacies (p. 191).** Technical, sociocultural and historical literacy.
- **Five lines of inquiry (pp. 192–193).** (1) history and historiography: AI "cannot simply be thought in terms of the present and an irrationally exuberant future"; (2) the human; (3) epistemology; (4) rhetoric and aesthetics; (5) interpretability and explainability, which ends by aligning interpretation in ML with "hermeneutics as it has been historically understood" (p. 193).
- The field "has settled to a certain extent on a set of ethicopolitical investments" (p. 192).

**Appendix contributors (each in their own voice):**

- **Caroline Bassett (pp. 193–194):** question the claim that AI delivers "control and freedom"; recover "lost histories of critique and refusal." Includes "questions concerning autonomy (which don't reduce to automation)" and "simulation and creation."
- **David M. Berry (pp. 194–195):** critique "magical thinking about computation." AI is "subsumed to the needs of capitalism." He calls for a "progressive left computationalism."
- **M. Beatrice Fazi (p. 195):** study computational processes as possible "modes of thought" in their own right, not as imitations of biological cognition.
- **Orit Halpern (pp. 195–196):** reads Hayek (1945) as a founding text. "AI is not a technology—it is an epistemology and also a form of governmentality."
- **Colin Milburn (pp. 196–197):** pedagogy. Teach students to use AI "more responsibly" and to "collaborate with AIs," aiming at "a more mutualistic relation."
- **Luciana Parisi (p. 197):** AI "menaces the autonomy of the humanities." Critiques the "Promethean promise" of self-determination and its "bioeconomical order." AI could open thinking toward "the inhumanities."

**Approach:** field-defining introduction and literature map, with interpretive readings of artworks and media (Ridler, Dinkins, Gibson's *Neuromancer*, the K-drama *Start-Up*, the *AlphaGo* documentary). It works mainly by historical and critical-theoretical framing.

## 3. The work the source does

Historical framing and field-mapping, with interpretation of creative practice as a form of critique. The appendix is a multi-voice survey, not a dialogue: the contributors do not respond to one another. On its own terms it is a "navigational guide" for readers (p. 193). By its own admission its density has "correlationist overtones" (p. 191).

## 4. Passages for closer reading

- **pp. 185–186.** The reading of Ridler's *Myriad (Tulips)* as historical counter to "presentism." It joins creative practice and historical framing in one example.
- **pp. 192–193.** The five lines of inquiry, especially (1) history and historiography and (4) rhetoric and aesthetics.

## 5. Claim, interpretation, open question

- **Authors' claim:** the humanities' contribution to AI is a critical, historically informed, multi-literate practice. Artworks that use ML self-reflexively are one model of it (pp. 186, 191).
- **Qualifications the authors keep:** "not all so-termed AI art does this political and aesthetic work" (n. 20). The field's "concepts and paradigms must remain unsettled" (p. 192). The term "AI" is used despite its "reductive, even absurd aspects" (p. 188).
- **My interpretation:** Raley and Rhee value Ridler's practice for its authorial closeness to the data she "collected and labeled herself" (p. 186). Chiodo (S02, p. 5) also cites Ridler, but as an artist using AI as a tool, whom she places outside her argument. Both value the human element in Ridler's practice, for different reasons. This is my comparison; neither source cites the other.
- **Open question:** the five lines of inquiry name ethics only indirectly ("ethicopolitical investments," p. 192). How does critical AI relate to applied AI ethics of the kind S01 proposes? Note 10 points to Luke Stark's essay in the issue on "ethics and ethical inquiry," which was not read.
