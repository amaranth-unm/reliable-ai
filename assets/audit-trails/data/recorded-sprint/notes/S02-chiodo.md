## 2. Argument, concepts, approach

**Author's claim.** "if AI's outputs are greatly autonomous from humans' inputs, there is no art" (p. 2). In art "not only the outputs but also the processes are essential," and what makes human processes "irreducibly different" is "meta-sensemaking," which AI "does not, and cannot, have" (p. 2).

**Steps of the argument:**

- **Scope (p. 2).** Chiodo excludes cases where artists use AI "as a tool among others," naming Ambrosi, Chung, McGregor and Ridler among others. She focuses on outputs "greatly autonomous" from human inputs, chiefly Obvious's *Edmond de Belamy* (sold at Christie's in 2018).
- **Shaping (pp. 3–4).** Using Obvious's own list of human versus AI actions, she argues that "the shaping itself of the artefact" has been distinctive of art "for millennia," across imitation, expression, historical and institutional definitions (Tatarkiewicz, Beardsley, Levinson, Dickie, Duchamp).
- **Artistic versus aesthetic (pp. 4–5).** Current discourse treats the artistic and the aesthetic as one thing, a "hendiadys" (p. 4). Boden's Turing test for art is "seminal" for this. Chiodo answers with the case of an aesthetically perfect copy of Picasso's *La Celestina* and argues the two are "partially (but meaningfully) divergent" (p. 4).
- **First thought experiment (pp. 5–7).** We can imagine Poe (*The Philosophy of Composition*) and Borges giving reasons for their works. Even Borges's "mysterious" reasons are still reasons. We cannot imagine Obvious or the AI doing the same. The etymology of *automatizo*, *automaton* and *Automatia* links automation to chance, "even the opposite of autonomy's self-determination" (p. 6). Autonomy, drawing on Kant, is "both an enormous opportunity and an enormous burden" (p. 6).
- **Second thought experiment (pp. 7–9).** An art historian reads *La Celestina* (Holloway 2006). Art is "sharing the first-hand experience of being human" (p. 8). "Meta" means "posteriority and additionality": the meaning added by humans reflecting on human existence (p. 9).
- **Conclusion (pp. 9–11).** *Edmond de Belamy* sold at an art price because we increasingly treat art as "absolute and self-referential shape," a trend with roots in the move from classical to romantic culture (Hegel) (p. 9). AI "may be read as what can unburden us from our autonomy's heaviest burdens" (p. 10). She proposes a "right to always know the authorship of the artefacts" (p. 10) and uses Goodman on forgery (p. 10). "Traditional" definitions can be "promising tools" for deciding eligibility (p. 11), and she extends this to not wanting "AI physicians and psychologists" (p. 11).

**Concepts:** meta-sensemaking; autonomy versus automation; the artistic versus the aesthetic; "wallpaper" (taken from Ridler, p. 5; see n. 13); unburdening; the right to know authorship.

**Approach:** philosophy of art and philosophy of technology. The method is conceptual argument, thought experiments, etymology and a sketch of the history of Western definitions of art (pp. 3–4, 9–10).

## 3. The work the source does

A conceptual distinction (artistic versus aesthetic; autonomy versus automation) argued through literary and art-historical interpretation (Poe, Borges, Picasso, Flaubert, Giotto, Rembrandt via Goodman). It also offers a cultural diagnosis of why AI art is valued. The historical framing is mostly etymological and long-range ("for millennia," classical to romantic). On its own terms the argument depends on the scope restriction to "greatly autonomous" outputs.

## 4. Passages for closer reading

- **pp. 5–6.** The Ridler quotation ("art without intent," "wallpaper") and the move to "the human element," then the Poe/Borges experiment and the etymology of automation. This is where the core distinction is made.
- **p. 10.** "AI may be read as what can unburden us from our autonomy's heaviest burdens." This is the ethical turn of the argument, with the "right to always know the authorship."

## 5. Claim, interpretation, open question

- **Author's claim:** autonomous AI artefacts cannot be art. Their appeal shows a cultural wish to be unburdened of meta-sensemaking (pp. 2, 10).
- **Qualifications the author keeps:** the claim covers only outputs "greatly autonomous from humans' inputs" (p. 2), not artists using AI as a tool. Sensemaking can be unconscious (p. 4, Surrealism; p. 6, Borges). A forger's copying might itself be a kind of meta-sensemaking (p. 10). She does not class wallpaper as art but notes it may sometimes be handicraft (n. 13). The unburdening is "understandable, and even touching" (p. 10), not simply condemned.
- **My interpretation:** Chiodo uses "burden" in a way opposite to S01's use (see [idea: burden](../ideas/burden-and-responsibility.md)). For her, the burden of autonomy is what makes art and responsibility possible, and relief from it is the problem. This is a thematic contrast I am drawing; neither author refers to the other.
- **Open question:** Obvious's own list of human actions (theme, research, data selection, tuning, choice of means: p. 3) is long. Is *Edmond de Belamy* a clear case of "greatly autonomous" output, or does the scope line sit where it needs to for the argument to work?
