Bring your own writing if you can: you'll learn more from a paragraph whose problems you already half-know. But if you'd rather not put your own work in, or you forgot, take one of these.
{: .lead}

**These are invented.** Nobody wrote them in earnest, and each has been built with the kinds of problems that turn up in real drafts — a claim arriving three sentences late, a term of art doing unexplained work, a pronoun with nothing to attach to, a policy that can't actually be enforced. Part of the exercise is finding out whether the machine notices the same ones you do.

## 1. A seminar paper paragraph

*For: students, and anyone who teaches writing.*

> The question of how communities responded to environmental change in the late nineteenth century has been addressed by numerous scholars, and there is a substantial body of literature on this topic. Some have argued that responses were largely reactive, while others have suggested a more complicated picture. In the case of the Middle Rio Grande, irrigation practices shifted considerably during this period, and this had significant effects on local social organization. Acequia associations, which had governed water distribution for generations, found themselves negotiating with new territorial authorities whose assumptions about property were fundamentally different. This suggests that the transition was not simply a matter of technological change but involved a renegotiation of who counted as a legitimate claimant to water — a shift that would shape the region's politics for the next century.

## 2. An abstract

*For: faculty and graduate students writing for journals or grant panels.*

> This paper interrogates the discursive construction of expertise in early twentieth-century public health campaigns, arguing that the professionalization of sanitary science cannot be understood apart from the racialized logics that structured its field of intervention. Drawing on an extensive archive of municipal records, professional correspondence, and popular ephemera, it demonstrates that the category of the "unsanitary" operated as a technology of governance well beyond its ostensible epidemiological function. The paper contributes to ongoing conversations in the history of medicine, critical race studies, and science and technology studies, and suggests that existing accounts have underestimated the extent to which these dynamics persist in contemporary public health infrastructures.

## 3. A syllabus policy

*For: faculty thinking about teaching. Run this one past the "confused student" reader.*

> **Use of AI Tools.** Students may use generative AI tools in this course where appropriate, but should not use them to complete assignments for them. Any use of AI should be disclosed. Work that is substantially produced by AI will not receive credit and may be referred as an academic integrity violation. Students are responsible for the accuracy of everything they submit. If you are unsure whether a particular use is permitted, ask before submitting. The goal of this policy is to support learning rather than to prohibit technology, and I expect students to use their judgment.

The syllabus policy is the one to try if you only have time for one. It looks perfectly clear until somebody reads it as a nervous nineteen-year-old at 11pm, and then it has roughly four unanswered questions in it. That gap — between what we think we've written and what someone else can actually do with it — is the thing this whole technique is for.
